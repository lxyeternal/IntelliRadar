
	   


A newly discovered scammer group from Nigeria dubbed Curious Orca has been observed while conducting manual target validation using blank emails to verify target information before launching their business email compromise (BEC) attacks.

BEC (which is also known as Email Account Compromise - EAC) fraud schemes are scams through which fraudsters trick one or more employees of a targeted organization into wiring money after posing as entities they trust like the company's CEO or trusted business partners, with the legitimate bank accounts were the funds would end being swapped beforehand with ones controlled by the crooks.

As part of the attack chain, the crooks have to first generate a list of employees they can target and validate the collected information to make sure that they all the data on the entity they decide to impersonate as part of the scam.

BEC attack chain

Agari Cyber Intelligence Division (ACID) found that the validation process which is part of the larger "lead validation and processing" step requires the scammers to carefully:


• validate the contact information to confirm that it is accurate,
• supplement the leads with open-source intelligence to identify additional information, such as the name of the CEO to be impersonated,
• organize the leads in a way that will allow the scammer sending the BEC emails to be more efficient.


Most BEC scammers use dedicated commercial services for lead generation which provide them with most if not all of the info needed to run their fraud schemes, including the employee's company title.

"Once a scammer conducts a customized search for corporate employees meeting their specific criteria, the service will provide a spreadsheet with target information, and even indicate whether their company has previously verified the email address," says ACID.

Validating lead information by hand

However, scam groups like Curios Orca are willing to put work extra to know for sure that the raw lead data they collected can be depended on once the BEC attack starts.

To do this, the crooks start from a list of potential targets within an organization, with names and possible email addresses. To check if the emails are legitimate, the fraudsters start probing every one of them by "sending a probing blank email with the subject “i” to the target to see if the email is delivered successfully."

Unlike the emails sent during active BEC campaigns, these reconnaissance emails will be sent outside working hours to avoid raising immediate suspicion.

Once they get sent, the criminals will wait patiently for a bounce notification from the email server of the targeted company, telling them that the email address they wanted to contact is not found.


Bounce notification example

If no bounce alert is received, the email address gets added to the list of valid emails, with the target's name, email address, and title being "added to one of the hundreds of consolidated text files containing verified targets."

As ACID notes, these files are also used to store as detailed as possible information on the targeted company's CEO to make the crooks' impersonation more accurate and believable.

Persistence is key in BEC scams

Even after a bounce notification is received by the Curious Orca scammers the validation process for that specific target doesn't end given that, base on the info at their disposal, the fraudsters will attempt to guess the username iterating through possible combinations based on the target's name and surname, which in some cases will help them stumble upon the correct address.

Only after sending blank probing emails for each of the username variations and receiving bouncing alerts for each of them will the scammers give up and excludes the target from the list used in subsequent BEC campaigns.

Although Curious Orca prefers to validate the raw target lead info by hand, "there are also online tools that attempt to predict the likely pattern used by email addresses on a specific domain," adds ACID.

These services would allow them to go through a raw list of leads a few orders of magnitude faster than a manual validation process and "easily spot patterns and then test the most likely email combination, without having to go through the manual testing outlined above."

While most people would not even think of going through the arduous email validation process used by Curious Orca, just one of the BEC group's associates "sent blank reconnaissance emails to more than 7,800 email addresses at over 3,200 companies in at least 12 countries including Australia, Canada, Denmark, Hong Kong, Israel, Italy, the Netherlands, Papua New Guinea, Singapore, Sweden, the U.K., and the U.S., since August 2018," as ACID found.


Curios Orca email validation work schedule

This amounts to a huge trove of targets added to the BEC scammers' master database, which at the moment features "more than 35,000 financial controllers and accountants at 28,000 companies around the world."

As ACID also discovered, some of the Curious Orca fraudsters seem to run their scammy activities following a schedule closely resembling a work schedule that employees of a legitimate business would abide by seeing that, in one of the cases monitored by ACID, a single individual spent roughly 46 hours doing reconnaissance by sending blank probing emails to potential targets.

ACID concludes their Curious Orca report by advising companies to disable email bounce notifications to external senders to block the scammers' bounce reconnaissance efforts or to set up inbound email filters that trigger alerts allowing employees that might be targeted by BEC fraudsters in the future to be a lot more careful when wiring money.

Highly profitable BEC fraud schemes

BEC scams were behind the highest reported total losses during last year for both companies and individuals, with victims losing over $1,2 billion in 2018 as detailed in an Internet Crime report published during April 2019 by FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3).

"Through the years, the scam has seen personal emails compromised, vendor emails compromised, spoofed lawyer email accounts, requests for W-2 information, and the targeting of the real estate sector," IC3's report says.


BEC SAR Filings monthly increases between 2016 and 2018

BEC frauds have also seen a staggering 476% growth between Q4 2017 and Q4 2018, with the number of BEC attacks targeting businesses and organizations increasing by 226% QoQ according to a Proofpoint report released in January.

The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) released a report in July asserting that BEC SAR filings (short for suspicious activity reports) expanded from an average of $110 million per month in 2016 to over $301 million per month in 2018. 

To make sure that employees are not going to get scammed by BEC fraudsters, organizations have to implement very strict vendor processes to check for and authenticate any changes via multiple processes, including face-to-face meetings and/or direct phone calls when having to make any changes to payment information.
