The practice of "SIM swapping" refers to crooks working with telco employees to port a user's phone number to a SIM card under an attacker's control for a short period.

During this time interval, the attacker uses access to the victim's phone number to log into the victim's online accounts that are protected with SMS-based one-time tokens.

There are countless such reports online, with users complaining that funds disappeared out of the blue, or they lost access to a Twitter or Instagram account without being able to do anything.

US authorities have started cracking down on such attacks, especially after it became apparent that this was becoming a growing threat on the criminal underground.

The first arrest

The first ever arrest for a SIM swapping attack took place last month, when the FBI arrested Joel Ortiz, a 20-year-old from Boston.

Authorities said Ortiz hacked at least 40 persons from whom he stole over $5 million worth of cryptocurrency. During his interrogation, Ortiz admitted to working with co-conspirators, who he said owned millions worth of cryptocurrency, just like him.

The second arrest

A few days later, authorities arrested a second man, Ricky Joseph Handschumacher, a 25-year-old from Port Richey, Florida. Investigators said they believed Handschumacher was part of a nine-man squad involved in similar SIM swapping attacks.

Handschumacher and his co-conspirators stand accused of using SIM swap attacks to break into the online accounts of several businessmen, including the CEO of the Gemini cryptocurrency exchange.

The third arrest

The latest arrest took place last week when California cops arrested Xzavyer Clemente Narvaez, a 19-year-old from Santa Clara.

According to court documents, Narvaez used SIM swapping attacks to steal over $1 million worth of cryptocurrency from people's online accounts.

Narvaez's deeds were discovered by an AT&T investigator who was looking into suspicious SIM transfers that have taken place on the company's network. Apple and Verizon helped investigators track down the suspect's IMEI number and his real-world identity.

Both Narvaez and Ortiz appear to be the members of an Internet forum named OGUSERS were SIM swappers congregated to brag and trade hacked accounts.
