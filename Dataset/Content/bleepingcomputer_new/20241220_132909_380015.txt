
	   


A cryptojacking campaign uses NSA's leaked DoublePulsar backdoor and the EternalBlue exploit to spread a file-based cryptocurrency malware on enterprise networks in China.

Dubbed Beapy by researchers at Symantec, the campaign was reported by other security companies before. Qihoo 360's research team published details about it and a Trend Micro report followed in mid-April.

However, information from Symantec adds details about the type of victims and attacker's motivation to use a file-based coinminer instead of the easier to deploy browser-based alternative.

The researchers saw the first signs of Beapy in January and that it "is most heavily affecting enterprises in Asia, with more than 80 percent of its victims located in China, with other victims in South Korea, Japan, and Vietnam."

Almost all of its activity focuses on enterprise environments, this sector recording a 98% infection rate.



The campaign starts with email phishing as the initial infection vector, luring the victim to open an attached Excel document that downloads the DoublePulsar malware; it allows the attacker to deliver the EternalBlue exploit that attacks the Microsoft Windows Server Message Block (SMB) protocol, enabling arbitrary code execution.

After the backdoor is installed, a PowerShell command makes the connection with Beapy's command and control (C2) server. More PowerShells are run before the coinminer is downloaded. EternalBlue is used to distribute the malware piece to vulnerable machines across the network.

Apart from the SMB exploit, Beapy also relies on Mimikatz post-exploitation tool that extracts Windows passwords. This way, the malware can be sent to patched systems, too.

Both DoublePulsar and EternalBlue were leaked to the public in 2017 by the Shadow Brokers group as part of a larger cache of tools employed by the US National Security Agency in its cyber operations.

Immediately after they lost their status, the tools were quickly adopted for destructive operations. The most infamous ones that involved EternalBlue are WannaCry and NotPetya. Little by little, various cybercriminal groups started adding them to their arsenal.

Web servers also a target

The researchers say that an earlier version of Beapy targeted a public-facing web server and tries to propagate to computers connected to them. This strain also includes Mimikatz and EternalBlue. Activity was first spotted in March and continued through early April.

A quick look on Shodan shows that there are over 500,000 public Samba servers available. Over 400,000 of them have authentication disabled.



In the web server compromise they saw, the researchers say that Beapy also tried to exploit a vulnerability (CVE-2017-5638) in Apache Struts and known flaws in Apache Tomcat (CVE-2017-12615) and the Oracle WebLogic Server (CVE-2017-10271).

Making money

Since Coinhive cryptomining service shut down in March, there was a drop in overall cryptojacking attacks. In February 2018, Symantec saw eight million cryptojacking attempts and the number fell to three million in March 2019. This improvement cannot be attributed to Coinhive's shuttering, but its dissolve definitely played a part.

The difference is that Coinhive offered browser-based cryptocurrency mining, which is much easier to pull as the victim machine needs only a browser and an internet connection. Beapy is a file-based miner, which needs to be planted on the compromised system in order to start minining.

Choosing this approach has the advantage of faster mining, turning the campaign into a more profitable endeavor.



"The Monero cryptocurrency, which is the cryptocurrency most commonly mined during cryptojacking attacks, dropped in value by 90 percent in 2018, so it may make sense that miners that can create more cryptocurrency faster are now more popular with cyber criminals," says Symantec.
