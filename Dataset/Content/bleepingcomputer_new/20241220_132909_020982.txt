
	   
Romanian authorities have arrested and extradited to the US three nationals suspected of creating and distributing the Bayrob malware.

US authorities said the three stole at least $4 million from their victims, but according to security firm Symantec, who helped track down the gang, the crooks might have stolen as much as $35 million.

Bayrob malware gang had three members

The gang's members are Bogdan Nicolescu (aka "Masterfraud," aka "mf"), Danet Tiberiu (aka "Amightysa," aka "amy"), and Radu Miclaus (aka "Minolta," aka "min").

All three were arrested in Romania earlier this year, and today, the US Department of Justice announced their extradition to the US, where they'll face charges of conspiracy to commit wire fraud, conspiracy to traffic in counterfeit service marks, aggravated identity theft, conspiracy to commit money laundering and 12 counts each of wire fraud.

Bayrob gang members [via Symantec]

The group came to be in 2007 when the crooks embarked on their cybercrime operation. Initially, their malware wasn't as complex as it is today, in its 8th generation.

In the beginning, the group focused on eBay and classified ads scams. They put up for sale fake products and awaited queries from potential buyers. The group usually sold expensive cars.

Group members would reach out to potential buyers, and negotiate a purchase. Victims would always receive a file, usually masked as a slideshow of photos depicting the vehicle put up for sale.

This file was laced with the Bayrob malware, which would infect the victim's PC when he would open the slideshow file.


Image slideshow app infected with Bayrob [via Symantec]

Bayrob malware showed fake eBay product pages

In its first versions, the Bayrob malware acted like a simplified banking trojan, but instead of showing fake login pages for banking portals, it would show fake eBay pages for non-existing products.

When the user would want to buy the product, they would be making a payment to the gang's bank accounts.

According to Symantec, the gang had the operation designed down to a pixel. Everything was perfect in the emails and fake eBay pages. The English was fluent and without mistakes, the eBay pages included realistic buyer feedback, and sometimes the Bayrob gang even created fake vehicle history reports and fake pages from escrow and delivery/trucking services.

The role of the escrow and trucking services was to delay customers as much as possible from finding out they were duped, and alert their banks or authorities.

Bayrob gang didn't shy away from robbing money mules

This gave the crooks time to use money mules and transfer the money out of the US, back to Romania. Symantec claims that in many cases, the gang didn't shy away from double-crossing the money mules, who were left waiting for their commissions.

In most cases, the vast majority of money mules had no clue they were recruited as part of a cybercriminal operation, most thinking they worked from home from legitimate companies, transferring funds and goods outside the US.

"Mules in the US were often vulnerable people in difficult circumstances," the Symantec team explains. "They appeared to be unwitting pawns of the gang, unaware of what they’d become involved with."

"Less is known about the money mules used in Europe," the security firm ads, "but most appeared to collect transferred funds using fake identities, indicating they may have been more complicit in the gang’s activities."


Money mule recruitment website set up by Bayrob gang [via Symantec]

Bayrob malware evolved into cryptocurrency miner

But eBay scams can get you so far. In a quest to boost their profits, around 2014, the gang started modifying their malware in order to collect credit card numbers and other information from infected computers, details which they used to make fraudulent purchases on their own, without having to trick victims into initiating the transfer.

Later on, and especially this year, Bayrob evolved from a backdoor trojan used for fraud into a cryptocurrency miner.

A remote observer would have figured out that something was wrong just by watching the network (botnet) of computers infected with Bayrob, which grew from around 1,000 computers in 2007, to around 50,000 bots in 2014, and up to 300,000 this year.

Bayrob gang had very solid OpSec

Symantec researchers say the Bayrob gang was extremely professional. In the beginning, when they targeted a few individuals at a time, the gang would take the time to compile a unique version of their malware, tailored for each victim.

As time evolved, and their infrastructure grew, they left a bigger footprint that researchers could follow. Around 2011, Symantec says it started to create a picture of Bayrob's backend infrastructure.

The security firm quickly tracked down the crooks back to Romania, and along with the FBI, kept track of their moves.

But these weren't your ordinary crooks. The gang used emails encrypted with PGPs and encrypted OTR (Off-The-Record) XMPP instant messaging to coordinate their "business" without being intercepted. The gang also used a double layer of proxies, one in Romania, and one in the US.

Symantec lay in hiding, watching these proxy servers for one year and a half until one of them leaked information that allowed investigators to track down each suspect.

After that, it didn't take long for this piece of crucial information to make its way down the chain of command. Symantec told the FBI, who phoned Romanian authorities, which in turn arrested the gang earlier this year.
