
	   


Password reuse is rampant among people aged 18 to 31, a category also referred to as millennials, according to a recent survey carried out by Keeper, the company behind the eponymous password manager application.

According to the Keeper's numbers, 87% of the people aged 18-31 acknowledged they reuse passwords, a practice long-time considered bad practice in the field of cyber-security.

The numbers aren't that different for people aged above 31, but they are a little better, as 81% said they admitted they reuse passwords across different accounts.

The Keeper survey, which compiled answers from 1,000 respondents, didn't only track password reuse between a user's own accounts, but also if he shared passwords with other persons in real-life, such as work colleagues, family members, or close friends.

According to the survey's results, only 64% said they do not share passwords with other persons. This number is actually very low since the point of having passwords is to safeguard data from other persons.

On the other hand, nearly a third of respondents said they shared passwords with at least two persons.

People still write passwords down on paper

Unfortunately, these are not the only bad practices the survey unearthed. According to collected data, three in four respondents relied on their memory, or on writing passwords down on paper to remember them.

This also explains why 33% of respondents said they failed login operations and often required three or four attempts before filling in the proper passwords.

More awkward is that 10% percent said they reset their password every time they log in, which is a somewhat safe technique, but quite time-consuming.

Password resets overall are not that uncommon, according to survey-takers, with 60% admitting they performed a password reset in the last two months. Of course, due to the large number of data breaches popping up here and there, it's hard to have a web life and not perform a password reset these days.



The survey, which focused mainly on mobile-related password security topics, also asked respondents if they used a password to protect their phone.

According to the Keeper survey, 26% of people aged 44 and younger, said they don't password-protect their phone, leaving it exposed to anyone that's in a position to pick it up.

This can lead to situations where attackers install malware on unprotected devices or thieves have easy access to the user's data.

People aged 44 and older were even less concerned with their phone's security, as 46% did not use passwords to protect their device.

Asked what device they consider the most secure and insecure, having to choose between phones, tablets, and desktops, respondents said they viewed desktops more secure and mobiles the most insecure.

Their answer is not a surprise since smartphones are still a relatively new technology, which most people still don't have a good grasp on like they do with desktop operating systems.
