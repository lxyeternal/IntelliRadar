Xu, who is a Chinese national, worked for IBM between November 2010 and May 2014, where he served as system software developer.

According to Xu's LinkedIn page, he was one of the many developers who worked on IBM's General Parallel File System (GPFS) technology, a high-performance clustered file system used in cloud hosting and with supercomputers.

The US Department of Justice says that before Xu left IBM in May 2014, the engineer made copies after the GPFS source code, which he later attempted to sell to third-parties.

One of those parties were two undercover FBI agents, who posed as financial investors who wanted to start a large-data storage technology company. Across several months Xu and the FBI agents had different conversations and meetings to arrange the sale.

These meetings came to fruition in March 2015, when Xu delivered a portion of the source code to two FBI agents. To disguise the software's true origin, Xu made modifications to the source code's GUI.

Nonetheless, the FBI verified the origin of Xu's source code with IBM engineers, who confirmed that this appeared to be a part of their GPFS technology.

Xu sold source code to China

After further negotiations between Xu and the undercover FBI agents, the FBI built a dummy network on which Xu later uploaded the entirety of the GPFS source code, as part of the sale process.

After IBM engineers confirmed that the source code Xu uploaded was, in fact, theirs, authorities arrested Xu on December 7, 2015, at a hotel in White Plains, New York.

Despite initially charging Xu with simple theft, in June 2016 authorities updated Xu's indictment to charges of economic espionage and theft of trade secrets after they discovered that Xu had sold the stolen source code to a third-party that wanted to use it for the National Health and Family Planning Commission of the People’s Republic of China.

After pleading guilty last week, Xu is now awaiting his sentencing, scheduled for October 13, 2017. The former IBM engineer faces up to 15 years in prison for each of the three counts of economic espionage, and up to 10 years for each of the three counts of theft of a trade secret.
