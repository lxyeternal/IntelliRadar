The average payment demand following a ransomware attack has almost doubled in the second quarter of the year and victims have Ryuk and Sodinokibi to blame.

The two pieces of malware have recorded increased activity lately, targeting organizations in the public and private sector.

Attacking this type of victims is more profitable as they are likely to pay a larger ransom to regain access to their files. This is because operational stagnation typically means a greater cost and reputational damage.

Two malware strains dominate the business

Data from ransomware incident response company Coveware paints a grim picture of the threats, showing that in Q2 2019 the average ransom payment increased by 184%, to $36,295. The mean for the previous quarter was $12,762.

Such incidents do not come without a downtime, though, and the period of a victim's inactivity grew from 7.3 days to 9.6 days lately.

The root cause for this change is a recent attack from Sodinokibi that infected managed service providers (MSPs) to push spread to managed endpoints.

Ryuk has been in thee limelight lately because it compromised the networks of public administrations in the US and getting paid, too, collecting hundreds of thousands of dollars from victims.

However, these targets made only 3.4% of all the ransomware victims in Q2, as the focus appears to be on larger outfits. Nevertheless, the public sector victims paid an average of $338,700, which is ten times more than the average paid by entities in the private sector.



Promise kept, some files still lost

According to the report from Coveware, 96% of the companies affected by ransomware that decided to pay the cybercriminals received a working decryption tool.

Both Ryuk and Sodinokibi make good on their promise to decrypt the data, as Coveware has yet to deal with an incident where the actors behind this malware failed to provide the decryption keys.

In the case of Ryuk, the tool is delivered two or three hours after the money; Sodonokibi relies on an automated TOR website.

Infosec and law enforcement organizations do not recommend paying the ransom as it only encourages the business model and attacks would become more frequent.

A good way to avoid becoming a victim is to implement a backup strategy that keeps files isolated from the main network. This ensures that the backup copies are safe even if malware spreads across the network.

Even with a decryption tool, some files may be lost forever. The company notes that in Q2 ransomware incidents some data could not be recovered, the average for this being around 8%.

This failure occurs mostly because of "of a flawed process where files are partially encrypted or wiped during the encryption process."

The recovery rate for Ryuk-encrypted files is 87%, while Sodinokibi's gets closer to 100%. Statistics vary for every ransomware strain.

Sharing the pie

As far as the market share goes, Ryuk became the most common threat for enterprises accounting for 23.9% of the ransomware incidents in Q2. It's victims are large companies with more 3,000 employees.

Dharma/Phobos targets smaller companies, with a share of 13.6%. It also focuses on companies, albeit smaller ones, with arounf 30 employees.

Sodinokibi seems to have replaced the defunct GandCrab service for the time being. Its piece of the pie is 12.5% and aims at businesses with about 80 employees.
