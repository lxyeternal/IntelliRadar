
	   


North Korean-backed hackers tracked as the Lazarus Group have developed and are actively using VHD ransomware against enterprise targets according to a report published by Kaspersky researchers today.

The researchers found VHD ransomware samples between March and May 2020 during two investigations, being deployed over the network with the help of an SMB brute-forcing spreading tool and the MATA malware framework (also known as Dacls).

"Functionally, VHD is a fairly standard ransomware tool. It creeps through the drives connected to a victim’s computer, encrypts files, and deletes all System Volume Information folders (thereby sabotaging System Restore attempts in Windows)," the report reads.

Links to North Korean hackers

While analyzing the two incidents, Kaspersky's researchers were able to determine the entire VHD ransomware infection chain starting with the attackers gaining access to their victims' network after successfully exploiting vulnerable VPN gateways.

Next, they escalated their privileges on the compromised devices and installed a backdoor, part of the multi-platform and modular MATA malware framework.

Kaspersky linked the MATA framework to the Lazarus hackers based on unique orchestrator filenames used in versions of the Manuscrypt trojan (also known as Volgmer).

Once the backdoor deployed, it allowed the attackers to take control of their victims' Active Directory server which made it possible to deliver VHD ransomware payloads to all systems on the network within 10 hours with the help of a Python-based loader.


VHD ransomware attack flow (Kaspersky)

Kaspersky attributed the VHD ransomware to the Lazarus Group based on the tools used to deploy the ransomware as part of the two attacks and the lateral movement tactics also observed in previous Lazarus intrusions.

"The data we have at our disposal tends to indicate that the VHD ransomware is not a commercial off-the-shelf product; and as far as we know, the Lazarus group is the sole owner of the MATA framework," Kaspersky said. "Hence, we conclude that the VHD ransomware is also owned and operated by Lazarus."

"We have known that Lazarus has always been focused on financial gain, however, since WannaCry we had not really seen any engagement with ransomware," senior security researcher at Kaspersky’s GReAT Ivan Kwiatkowski said.

"While it is obvious that the group cannot match the efficiency of other cybercriminal gangs with this hit-and-run approach to targeted ransomware, the fact that it has turned to such types of attacks is worrisome."

Financially motivated North Korean hackers

Lazarus Group (also tracked as Zinc by Microsoft and HIDDEN COBRA by the United States Intelligence Community) used the MATA malware framework to compromise and to deploy VHD ransomware payloads on the systems of companies from various industry verticals.

Some of the North Korean hackers tracked under the Lazarus moniker are well-known for being financially motivated as shown by their previous campaigns — the hack of Sony Films in 2014 as part of Operation Blockbuster and the 2017 global WannaCry ransomware epidemic.

Since 2007, Lazarus hackers have launched attacks against financial orgs from a multitude of countries including but not limited to South Korea, Taiwan, India, Mexico, Pakistan, Philippines, Turkey, Chile, and Vietnam, as well as on several cryptocurrency exchanges and targets in the aerospace, engineering, government, media, and technology industry sectors.

To put a light on the scale of the financial losses, experts with the United Nations (UN) Security Council say that the North Koreans were behind cryptocurrency heists that led to losses of $571 million during 2017 and 2018, with the U.S. Treasury signing sanctions against three DPRK-sponsored hacking groups (Lazarus, Andariel, and Bluenoroff) in September 2019.

Two Chinese nationals were later charged during March 2020 with the laundering over $100 million worth of cryptocurrency out of the approximately $250 million stolen by the Lazarus Group in 2018 alone as part of a single cryptocurrency exchange hack,

One month later, the U.S. government issued guidance on North Korean hacking activity, offering a reward of up to $5 million for any info on DPRK hackers' cyber activity, including past or ongoing operations if it leads to the disruption of DPRK-related illegal activities or the identification or location of North Korean actors.
