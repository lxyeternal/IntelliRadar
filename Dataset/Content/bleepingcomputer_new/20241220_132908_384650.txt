
	   


Twitter has disclosed a 'Data Security Incident' that caused the billing information for Twitter advertisers to be stored in the browser's cache. This bug would have allowed other users on the computer to see this data.

In February, Twitter disclosed that one of their APIs could have been abused by ‘nation state' actors to match usernames of Twitter accounts against phone numbers.

Now, this security incident has put the spotlight on the company again.

Twitter 'Data Security Incident' email
Source: Bleeping Computer

The information incorrectly being stored in the browser cache by this bug includes email addresses, phone numbers, last four digits of credit cards, and billing address.

"We became aware of an issue that meant that prior to May 20, 2020, if you viewed your billing information on ads.twitter.com or analytics.twitter.com the billing information may have been stored in the browser’s cache. Examples of that information include email address, phone number, last four digits of your credit card number (not complete numbers, expiration dates or security codes), and billing address. If you used a shared computer, it is possible that if someone used the computer after you, they could have seen the information stored in the browser's cache (most browsers generally store data in their cache by default for a short period of time like 30 days)," the data security incident email stated.

The email notification further warned users who may have used a shared computer as web browsers typically employ month-long caching periods.

"If you used a shared computer, it is possible that if someone used the computer after you they could have seen the information stored in the browser's cache (most browsers generally store data in their cache by default for a short period of time like 30 days)."

While the bug was fixed May 20th 2020, the data incident notification came today, over a month later, keeping users in the dark as to how long their information was exposed via this caching issue. 

As a fix, the company revised its code to prevent web browsers from caching sensitive information (screenshots below).

"On May 20, 2020, we updated the instructions that Twitter sends to your browser's cache to stop this from happening."

As of now, nothing is indicating that the sensitive information was compromised. However, no indication of compromise doesn't always equate to guarantee of no compromise.

The email notification suggests that as a safety precaution, users clear out their web browser caches:

"While we have no evidence that your billing information was compromised, we want to make sure you're aware of the issue and how to protect yourself going forward. If you currently use a shared computer to access your Twitter Ads or Analytics billing information, we recommend clearing the browser cache when you log out." 

The company has apologized for the mishap that happened and reassured users of the company's commitment to earning users' trust.

"We're very sorry this happened. We recognize and appreciate the trust you place in us, and are committed to earning that trust every day. If you have additional questions, you can write to our Office of Data Protection here."

Taking a closer look

Looking at the network requests that return sensitive JSON fields (complete billing address, Twitter account information, tax residence country, etc.), it appears that the fix has indeed been applied.

The responses are now sent with  "cache-control: no-store" headers instructing web browsers strictly not to cache this information. 

For example, below are the JSON Fields returned by the `tax_settings` API endpoint. As you can see, it contains quite a bit of information about the business customer.


Data returned by the tax_settings API

This endpoint is now using the 'cache-control: no-store' header to prevent this information from being cached.


Using the cache-control header

Overall, the risks of this data being seen by malicious actors were reasonably low as it would have required access to the computer to view the data, and full credit cards were not exposed.

The higher risk would have been if malware developers had known of this bug and used it to steal the info from the cache.

There is no indication that this bug had been abused.
