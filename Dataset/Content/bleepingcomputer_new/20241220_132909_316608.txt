At Symantec's behest, Google has removed three Android apps from the Play Store because these apps used various tricks to fool security scanners and deliver unwanted ads to the people that installed them.

All apps posed as battery improvement apps, mimicking the likes of Battery Doctor and Clean Master, two very popular Android apps that are said to help improve and prolong a phone battery's life. The app's titles, package names, and install count are as follows:

Clear Master Boost and Clean - com.sarabase.clearmaster.speedbooster (5,000 - 10,000)
	Fast Charge 2017 - com.desive.fastercharger.fastcharger (10,000 - 50,000)
	Fast Charger X3 Free - com.qt.fastercharger (10,000 - 50,000)
Apps delayed malicious behavior to fool AV scanners

According to Symantec, these apps used a series of tricks that made it near impossible for most security products to detect their malicious actions using simple scanning procedures.

For example, the apps used a delayed attack mechanism to start any malicious actions. This is not only very efficient but in the last months, has also been observed and used by more and more apps.

These malware apps, detected by Symantec under the generic Android.Fakeapp name, will wait for hours after being installed, and once the timer ends, will request their command-and-control (C&C) server a list of ads or apps to show or install on the contaminated device.

Apps renamed their process as "android"

Another trick, albeit seen in the wild before, is self-renaming. For example, the apps titled Fast Charger would rename their process name as "android."

Users inspecting the Settings/Apps section of their phone would have no idea which of the 2-3 services titled "android" is the real OS, and would most likely leave them alone, fearing not to crash their phone.

Furthermore, the apps also asked users for administrator rights. Since these are apps with "battery saving" features, some users would be comfortable giving these types of apps a bigger leash.

In this case, the adware apps would be even harder to remove, as they would gain access to persistence mechanisms that would ensure automatic reinstallation after phone reboots.

Most of these techniques have been seen in many other apps, but never together in such banal threats such as mere adware apps.

The presence of such code in basic adware suggests the commoditization of this type of attack techniques and the appearance of more advanced methods that are now being deployed for more potent malware such as spyware and banking trojans.
