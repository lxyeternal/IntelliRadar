Only last year, Apple announced this new “privacy tool” to come to every iPhone and apps, in its quest to offer greater security to Apple users. The sign-in feature was introduced to let users log in to services with their Apple ID, as opposed to an email address and password.

This new service was promoted as a 'more private way" to sign into apps and websites as you can use the Apple ID you already have created rather than creating a new one.

Apple also promised not to track the apps and websites you log in to and only to retain information necessary to sign you in.

"Sign in with Apple won’t track or profile you as you use your favorite apps and websites, and Apple retains only the information that’s needed to make sure you can sign in and manage your account," Apple explains in a support bulletin.

As is common with new authentication services with a huge user base, it was quickly adopted by well-known apps and websites, including Spotify, Dropbox, Airbnb, among many others.

Vulnerability allowed sign-in from any Apple ID

In April, full-stack developer and researcher Bhavuk Jain discovered a critical flaw in the OAuth-styled “Sign in with Apple” feature that could let a hacker sign in using any Apple ID.

For the discovery and coordinated disclosure, Apple has awarded Jain $100,000 as a part of its bug bounty program.

Apple's promoted privacy controls are futile when this vulnerability allowed "a full account takeover of user accounts on that third party application irrespective of a victim having a valid Apple ID or not," as Jain explained in his blog post.


Source: Bhavuk Jain

In simplest terms, the authentication token (JWT token) being generated by the Apple server, when signing in to a service is tied to a user’s Apple ID.

However, the actual workflow involved with logging the user into the service fails to check whether the person using the authentication token is the rightful owner of the Apple ID.

“This means an attacker could forge a JWT by linking any Email ID to it and gaining access to the victim’s account,” stated Jain.

You can imagine how damaging this vulnerability could have been if exploited. Of course, the company has patched the flaw, and an internal audit of their logs has revealed no signs of compromised accounts.

Jain’s detailed findings and public disclosure of the vulnerability are published on his blog.
