
	   


Germany and France feel the makers of encrypted messaging apps like Telegram and Signal should help fight Islamic State terrorists, and they're pushing for Europe-wide rules to make that happen.

On 23 August, French Interior Minister Bernard Cazeneuv and his German counterpart Thomas de Maiziere (shown in the images below, respectively) issued a joint proposal that outlines how European governments can work with encrypted services to fight the Islamic State. 

According to AP News, the two ministers said they have no desire to ban encrypted apps or products. They would instead like to see the creation of a framework by which technology companies can help prevent terrorists from using their products and hand over encrypted conversations to law enforcement in the event they detect suspicious activity.

"Encrypted communications among terrorists constitute a challenge during investigations. Solutions must be found to enable effective investigation ... while at the same time protecting the digital privacy of citizens by ensuring the availability of strong encryption."




Such a framework would empower law enforcement to "eavesdrop" on encrypted communications, capabilities which many in the national security community deem are essential in the fight against the Islamic State. For example, both ministers argue that law enforcement could have used the framework to cooperate with Telegram and stop a 19-year-old who attacked a Catholic church in Normandy in July.

The teenage terrorist used the encrypted messaging app to post details about his plans. Telegram told AP News that it blocks terrorist communications on public channels, but it doesn't interfere in private messages.

Cazeneuv and de Maiziere intend to follow up their proposal with a discussion on encryption and other security issues at a summit in Bratislava, Slovakia in September. There they will highlight specific points of their plan, including the idea that visitors to Europe should register with a eligibility system similar in function to the United States' Electronic System for Travel Authorization (ESTA).

It remains to be seen whether the two ministers' efforts, which follow on the heels of Germany's creation of a cyber unit, will fare better than similar campaigns launched by authorities in the United States. What immediately comes to mind is the dispute between Apple and the FBI over the iPhone of one of the San Bernardino shooters, who also professed his commitment to IS. The FBI ultimately got what it wanted by hiring external hackers to disable a security feature on the device. But no one would say the FBI "cooperated" with Apple, despite the fact that other U.S. tech companies said they would have done so if it had been them.

In the meantime, one can only assume privacy advocates will not take kindly to this news.
