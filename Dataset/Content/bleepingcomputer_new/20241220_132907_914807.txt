
	   
Security researchers from WhiteScope have uncovered over 8,600 vulnerabilities in pacemaker systems and the third-party libraries used to power various of their components.

These issues are broad in nature and range from simple code bugs to terrible design choices that risk the life of their patients.

Researchers discovered these flaws in seven different products from four different manufacturers. These issues are detailed in depth in a report the team released earlier this week.

Most vulnerabilities are in third-party libraries

The focus on their research was on radio-controlled implantable devices such as pacemakers, Implantable Cardioverter Defibrillators (ICD), Pulse Generators, and Cardiac Rhythm Management (CRM) — collectively referred to as "pacemaker systems" in this article.

Conducting a security audit of this whole infrastructure resulted in the discovery of over 8,600 vulnerabilities associated with outdated libraries used for the software of pacemaker programmers.



Basic design flaws expose pacemaker systems to hacking

Furthermore, there are also basic design issues that pacemaker system vendors need to address.

For example, pacemaker programmers and the implanted pacemaker from the same vendors don't authenticate each other, allowing a malicious party that gets close enough to a victim to alter the implant's settings.

Similarly, pacemaker programmers don't require physicians to authenticate on the device, which means a third party can steal one of these devices and use them to do harm, having no worry of not being able to access the programmer's interface.

Last but not least, pacemaker systems stored data on unencrypted filesystems on removable media, meaning anyone can pick up one of these systems and start learning how to hack them.

Implantable medical devices known to be insecure since 2013

As is typical with such reports that raise awareness to an issue, the pacemaker vendors have not been named. Nonetheless, researchers said they contacted ICS-CERT and forwarded their findings so the organizations could address the security flaws in private.

The fact that pacemakers and other implantable medical devices feature glaring security holes is already well known. Since 2013 there have been numerous warnings from both ICS-CERT and the FDA. The FDA also issued a guide on how to secure medical devices.

Nonetheless, security researchers have still found vulnerabilities in these devices in the years that followed, including in insulin pumps, not just pacemakers.
