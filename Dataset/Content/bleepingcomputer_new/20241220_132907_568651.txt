
	   


A website surfaced online today, posing to be the infamous LeakedSource data hoarding service, which went down shrouded in mystery at the end of January 2017.

The original LeakedSource launched in late 2015, and it became known worldwide after it disclosed mega data breaches affecting services such as LinkedIn, MySpace, Dropbox, and many others.

Wannabe hackers loved LeakedSource

At the same time, the site also became extremely popular with amateur hackers because, for a small fee, it allowed anyone to view cracked passwords from user accounts included in these breaches.

That's why when LeakedSource went down, many were glad to see it go.

The original LeakedSource went down on January 25, but despite the endless stream of news stories you've read about getting raided by the FBI, the information was never confirmed by authorities, and the service's downfall remained a mystery to this day.

There have been other LeakedSource copycats

Since then, one Twitter account attempted to pose as the official LeakedSource account, but when Bleeping Computer reached out, they failed to answer a simple question about themselves.

The question was "why was the first LeakedSource Twitter account banned?" Despite claiming to be the original LeakedSource team, the copycats didn't know the answer, which was that Twitter had banned LeakedSource's original account in mid-September 2016 after the service indexed around 32 million records holding information on Twitter users, most likely obtained from malware that logged users credentials.

LeakedSource.ru portal launched today

Today, while navigating underground hacking forums for new malware releases, your reporter stumbled upon a conversation where forum users were debating a newly launched version of LeakedSource.

This new website, currently hosted at LeakedSource.ru, claims to be a continuation of the official LeakedSource service. The following statement was included on the newly launched website.

February 19th, 2017
Due to recent developments, we are required to switch server providers and domain name providers. Due to this unexpected course of events, we will be missing some content and we no longer have access to our old servers and old domain names, but we are working vigorously to restore LeakedSource to it's original glory. LeakedSource will now be running under the ccTLD .ru.
Just as a reminder to all of our users, we take your privacy seriously. As such, we feel like it's our due deligence to inform you that we do not store personal information on our servers.
Finally we'd like to thank you, the users and supporters, for your patience in this process.
NOTE: Everyone will have to register again.
NOTE: Every user will have to purchase a subscription again.
NOTE: Businesses that used our API will have to purchase access to it again.
Regards,
LeakedSource



Your reporter had several conversations with the original LeakedSource team and had worked together to verify past data breaches on several occasions.

When we reached out today via Twitter, the LeakedSource.ru team said they were aware of our past conversations, which isn't a revelation since they were publicly disclosed in multiple articles, but declined to provide any information from previous discussions.

Asked to elaborate on the first sentence of their announcement, the one about "switch[ing] server providers and domain name providers," the LeakedSource.ru team said they "decided it was for the best because we had at least one person from the team that left the project."

Regarding the unconfirmed FBI raids, the team had only the following to say: "As for the unconfirmed reports they are just speculations, so there isn't much to elaborate on it."

Is it a scam, or is LeakedSource back?

What we know until now, is that the new LeakedSource.ru website isn't a phishing site, as it grants access to data breach records, according to one user who ventured and purchased a new account on the new LeakedSource.

"I ordered and it's not a phising [sic] site but not the same content so I don't know if it's the same owner," said a Twitter user going by the name of @dotnet_sharp.

Users on HackForums, a meeting place for hackers and malware authors, also spotted the same thing, pointing out big differences between this new version and the old LeakedSource.



Despite this criticism, the LeakedSource.ru team said in a private Twitter discussion that their service was not yet fully restored. The LeakedSource.ru team offered this information before @dotnet_sharp posted his observation on Twitter, and even asked for Bleeping Computer to delay the article until they fully restore the service.

The counter and the Russian domain

What was more curious was that LeakedSource.ru lists a counter of breached accounts on its homepage. This number is "3,109,103,084" which is the exact same total indexed by the Wayback Machine in an archive of the original LeakedSource website taken on January 12.

In previous conversations between the original LeakedSource crew and your reporter, the team was very proud about their counter, which they updated quite often.

It's hard to believe the LeakedSource team didn't update their counter between January 12 (Wayback Machine snapshot) and January 25 (original LeakedSource went down).

Furthermore, hosting their new site on a Russian domain may have not been such a great idea. LeakedSource hosted data from many Russian-based services, such as Mail.ru, VK.com, Rambler.ru, and others.

A scam or not, if LeakedSource.ru lists data from any Russian service, the domain won't last very long. Russia's pretty sensitive about these types of things.

Many consider LeakedSource.ru an FBI honeytrap

After your reporter posted a tweet announcing the appearance of this LeakedSource clone, many security researchers and people involved in the cybercrime scene reached out with their opinions.

There were two general trains of thought. The first is based on the fact that the FBI had truly raided LeakedSource's servers back in January, and is now hosting a honeytrap that's trying to catch people who register on the site. The thinking is that the FBI is attempting to gather evidence or new leads on persons who engage in cybercriminal activities, as they most likely already have access to the original LeakedSource servers and its customerbase.

As we said before, the site offered access to cracked passwords, which most teenage hackers used to access social media accounts for their friends, rivals, or famous celebrities.

Many also fear LeakedSource.ru is just a money grab

The second is that someone is running a scam, capitalizing on LeakedSource's brand and past userbase, hence the request that all users should re-register and re-purchase a subscription. It might be that a person or group is trying to collect as much money as possible before disappearing into the blue.

To be honest, it's not really that hard to set up a website like LeakedSource.ru in the first place. For example, in October 2016, your reporter was tipped off about a possible data breach of hacking forum Exploit.in.

After your reporter downloaded the data to verify its content, the archive actually contained around one billion records from past data breaches such as VK.com, LinkedIn, MySpace, and others.

The investigation showed that no data was ever leaked from Exploit.in, but only data stolen by one hacker from another, and leaked publicly as part of a personal dispute the two were having. This was confirmed by the hacker who leaked it.

At the time, LeakedSource had around 2 billion records, so someone leaked around half of LeakedSource's database without even realizing it.

The conclusion of this faux Exploit.in data breach is that there are many people on the hacking underground that are in possession of many of these leaked databases, so assembling a LeakedSource clone overnight isn't that hard as you'd imagine.

The general consensus in the security world is that LeakedSource.ru is a scam... but only when trying to pass as the original LeakedSource.

As @dotnet_sharp has shown Bleeping Computer in a private conversation, the service does list details from past data breaches.
