
	   


Memory modules with error-correcting code (ECC) protection are vulnerable to Rowhammer, an attack that can help corrupt data the computer stores in its volatile memory chips.

Since its discovery in 2014, Rowhammer attacks were thought to be possible against regular dynamic random access memory (DRAM) available in most computers on the market.

The technique exploits a design flaw in the way memory cells that store the data are organized in a DRAM module. Because the cells are too close together, the researchers found that if they bomb the same area with enough read-write operations, they can modify the in-memory information.

ECC protection has its limitations

Until recently, the ECC mechanism present in memory modules for servers and high-end systems was believed to provide good protection against Rowhammer because of its role to detect and correct single-bit soft memory errors that occur by accident.

A research team from  VUSec, the security group at Vrije University in Amsterdam, Netherlands, found that although ECC is an obstacle in running a Rowhammer attack, it is not insurmountable.

After a year of testing and studying how multiple ECC implementations worked, VUSec was able to grasp the details of the mechanism and to devise an efficient bypass method they called ECCploit.

ECC can correct only one bit flip at a time and if two of them occur in the same memory segment, the mechanism crashes the system.

"Only if you have three bitflips in the right places, will you be able to bypass ECC," the VUSec team writes in a blog post, emphasizing that the trick is to avoid flipping two bits to prevent a system crash.

Checking the bits

Their technique requires that the bits in the hammered location and in the target area are all the same, except one. This information can be obtained by flipping bits one at a time and checking if the time needed to read from that particular location increases, indicating that a correction occurred.

In their research paper, VUSec explains that their method does not require physical access as it can work from an unprivileged remote shell.

Despite the risk it poses to high-end machines, pulling ECCploit can be very time-consuming. According to VUSec, using the initial approach of the attack could take between 32 minutes and one week to find exploitable bit flips.

ECCploit is a timing side-channel variation of Rowhammer that is currently tracked as CVE-2018-18904. All this makes the attack a theoretical threat, at least for the foreseeable future.
