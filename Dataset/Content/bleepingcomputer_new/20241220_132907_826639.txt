
	   


Intel processors are vulnerable to a new attack known as SGAxe that breaches the security guarantees of Intel Software Guard eXtensions (SGX) enclaves. It is designed to specifically target and leak data from Intel processors.

SGAxe is an evolution of the CacheOut attack (also known as L1D Eviction Sampling) previously disclosed by researchers at the University of Michigan, University of Adelaide, and Data61 in January 2020.

CacheOut is tracked as CVE-2020-0549 and it may allow authenticated attackers with local access to the targeted machines to potentially enable information disclosure due to cleanup errors in some data cache evictions for some Intel processors.

Extracting protected data from SGX enclaves

The SGAxe attack allows extracting SGX attestation keys from Intel’s quoting enclave, making it possible for network attackers to cryptographically impersonate legitimate SGX Intel machines.

"In the SGAxe paper we do the same for attestation keys, basically recovering the entire key from Intel’s production SGX enclaves," the researchers explain.

They used the SGAxe and CacheOut attacks to steal private attestation keys from up to date and trusted SGX machines.


SGX’s Attestation Process

"With these private attestation keys in hands, we can sign arbitrary SGX attestation quotes which are then considered legitimate by Intel’s attestation service (or at least until they revoke our key)," the researchers explain.

Attestation keys are issued by Intel's hardware-based attestation services to software running within an Intel SGX enclave to seal secrets and unseal the secrets later.

A video demo of the researchers signing an arbitrary quote for remote attestation and verify it against Intel’s Attestation Service is embedded below.



SGAxe mitigation

Intel will mitigate CacheOut and SGAxe by providing CPU microcode updates to OEM vendors to patch the root cause. 

These updates will then be delivered to end-users as BIOS updates, with Intel to also perform a Trusted Compute Base (TCB) recovery to invalidate all previous signed and compiled attestation keys.

"This process will ensure that your system is in a secure state such that your system is able to use remote attestation again," the researchers add.

However, since these are bugs in the processor's silicon, "[s]oftware can mitigate these issues at the cost of features and/or performance."

Intel will have to release in-silicon fixes addressing these issues for newer processor generations not to take a similar performance hit because of the software mitigation.

Most Intel processors vulnerable to attacks

The attack can be used against machines using Intel’s 9th generation Coffee Lake Refresh processors, fully updated with all the SGX countermeasures Intel published so far.

SGAxe only affects Intel's processors as its platform is the only one offering the Intel SGX feature. Also, there is no evidence that the attack was used in the wild until its public disclosure.

The SGAxe vulnerability will be mitigated in the same microcode update designed to mitigate the CacheOut speculative execution attack. 

The full list of processors potentially affected by SGAxe was published by Intel's Product Security Incident Response Team (PSIRT) here.

"The CacheOut researchers recently informed us of a new paper referred to as SGAxe," Intel Director of Communications Jerry Bryant said today.

"It is important to note that SGAxe relies on CVE-2020-0549 which has been mitigated in microcode (confirmed by the researchers in their updated CacheOut paper) and distributed out to the ecosystem."

More details and technical information on the SGAxe attack are available in the research paper titled "SGAxe: How SGX Fails in Practice."
