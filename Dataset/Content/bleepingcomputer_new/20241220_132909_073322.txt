
	   


Vulnerabilities found in networking gear from D-Link and Comba Telecom allow retrieving sensitive information like ISP credentials and device access passwords without authentication.

For some of the products, the information is present in the source code of the web-based management interface.

Easy to reach passwords

Looking at the D-Link DSL-2875AL router, Simon Kenin of Trustwave found that it is affected by a vulnerability that was reported in the past for other products from the same manufacturer.

Kening found a second vulnerability leading to sensitive information disclosure that impacts D-Link's DSL-2875AL as well as the DSL-2877AL model.

Simply reading the HTML code on the login page of the two devices reveals two lines corresponding to the credentials for authenticating with the Internet Service Provider (ISP).



In an advisory today, the researcher warns that an attacker could "use those credentials for himself and abuse the ISP."

D-Link received a report about these flaws at the beginning of January and released patches on September 6. It is worth noting that DSL-2875AL is marked as discontinued in some regions of the world.

The manufacturer released fixes for both DSL-2875AL and DSL-2877AL.

Comba Telecom keeps silent

Kening also found multiple remote and password disclosure issues in products from Comba Telecom, a company advertising itself as "a supplier of infrastructure and wireless enhancement solutions to mobile operators and enterprises to enhance and extend their wireless communications networks."

The vulnerable products are the AC2400 WiFi access controller, and the AP2600-I-A02 and the AP2600 indoor access points. It's important to note that these products are typically intended for offices or spaces that need to extend WiFi coverage or to handle a large number of users; AC2400, for instance, "can manage 512 11n fit APs and 32,000 wireless users."

Getting sensitive data from them is possible without authentication by sending a crafted request to the web management server and downloading the configuration file; or by looking at the HTML page source of the web-based administration console.

Depending on the vulnerability they leverage, an attacker may have to crack an MD5 hash of the password; this is trivial these days, though. All the details are available in an advisory from the security company.

If D-Link responded to the disclosure and released a patch for their devices, attempts to contact Comba Telecom were unsuccessful. Trustwave tried multiple time to reach out to the company starting February 15 but received no reply.
