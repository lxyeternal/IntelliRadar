
	   


Agents of the Information Network Security Agency (INSA) - the top-level cyber security agency in Ethiopia, used laughingly weak credentials to protect their email accounts.

The revelation comes from a database that did not use encryption to protect sensitive data from internal or external threats. It is alleged that a disgruntled employee with access the information leaked the database before they left the agency.

This shows that sensitive data should be protected from internal threats as well as external ones.

Discovered by researchers at SafetyDetective, the database listed credentials for 300 email accounts and many of them were protected with passwords that a brute-force attack would find in a blink of an eye.

According to the report, 142 accounts could be unlocked by typing in "p@$$w0rd," and there were three instances where agents used "password" to log in.

Judging from their length, INSA did not impose a minimum number of characters for an acceptable password as several of them were four or five-characters long. And even the longer ones would be easy prey since they lacked any complexity.



According to the Ethiopian publication The Reporter, the leak is the result of political instability in the country, which caused a reform at INSA that ousted "a number of key cyber-security agents."

A member of INSA's technical team told the publication that the leak was almost a year old and it clearly comes from a former agent that copied the emails and passwords from the admin panel.

One password sums it up nicely

Explaining the unusual number of "p@$$w0rd" strings, they said that this is the default password the admin issues to new email accounts. Even if those are new, some passwords suggest accounts as old as 2009 - INSA was founded in 2008.

Others are a random string of alphanumerical characters (just numbers and letters) while some add a symbol or two, weaker even than the default pick.

But the list also includes a password that portrays the security state of the server. That is "jesushelpme."

INSA has reset the passwords and has implemented some "quick fixes" that addressed "some of the obvious security vulnerabilities," says the agency's communication director, Solomon Tesfaye; otherwise, a lot more could have been exposed, Tesfaye added.

"As the most tech-savvy people in Ethiopia, whose entire careers literally revolve around online and national security, their lack of secure passwords is absolutely shocking," says SafetyDetective.

Encryption of sensitive data both at rest and in transit, protection against brute-force attacks, and implementing a strong-password policy should be part of the minimum protections an organization implements for their internal data.
