Camouflaged as utility apps or games, Mobstspy infected devices primarily in India, although its distribution was worldwide, affecting users in 196 countries.

Their names are HZPermis Pro Arabe, Flappy Bird, Win7Launcher, Win7imulator, FlashLight, and Flappy Birr Dog.

Stats for most of them showed they were installed up to 100 times, with two exceptions:
- Win7Launcher, up to 5,000 installations
- Win7imulator, up to 500,000 installations

Apps that port the Windows theme to an Android phone (as shown in the picture above) are bound to enjoy huge popularity.

Phishing for Google and Facebook logins

The author(s) behind Mobstspy included phishing capabilities that allow it to launch a fake login screen whenever the victim tries to access their Facebook or Google account.

Most users would not see through the forged screen and are very likely to fall for the trick. When they provide their username and password for the first time, the malware shows them a failed login attempt, but the credentials have already been stolen.



Unless users protect access to their accounts with the two-factor authentication (2FA) feature, their accounts are easily compromised this way. Both Facebook and Google have support for 2FA.

Malware sets sight on all valuable data

Unlike run-of-the-mill spyware, Mobstspy is interested in a wider range of data present on the compromised devices.

Apart from lifting contacts, call logs or text conversations, it is also fitted with a list of folders to search for images and recorded voice conversations stored by different apps.

The content type it targets can be anything from audio, video, images, or documents, and it looks in folders belonging to specific apps, such as WhatsApp, Messenger, Snapchat, Viber, and call recording apps.

Even the Bluetooth storage location, the phone's camera and sounds directories are on the list.



File delivery method

Malware researchers from Trend Micro identified the command and control servers (C2) where Mobstspy sends all the information: mobistartapp[.]com, coderoute[.]ma, hizaxytv[.]com, and seepano[.]com.

To deliver the information, the malware relied on Firebase Cloud Messaging, developed by a Google subsidiary as a free cloud solution for messages and notifications for Android, iOS, and web applications.

"Once the malicious application is launched, the malware will first check the device’s network availability. It then reads and parses an XML configure file from its C&C server," the researchers explain in a blog post today.

The spyware gathers device-related information that helps the attackers keep evidence of the infected devices and target only specific devices in future campaigns.



Once this data is delivered to the C2, the device is registered and the malware can receive commands through the Firebase messaging service, and execute them.

Trend Micro researchers say that the malicious apps were available for download in 2018 and Google suspended five of them in February last year. The last one of them, probably the one with the highest number of installations, resisted in Play store for an unspecified period but it is no longer available in Android's official marketplace.

Despite Google's cleanup, these apps are still available in third-party Android app stores and users lured by the promised cosmetic changes or games can still infect their phones with Mobstspy.
