
	   


For the first time ever, researchers have detected an Android botnet leveraging a Twitter command-and-control (C&C) account to manage communications across all enslaved devices.

Lukáš Štefanko, a malware researcher at Slovakian security firm ESET, is responsible for having discovered the botnet, which goes by the name, "Android/Twitoor."

As in most malware attacks, Android/Twitoor doesn't show up on a user's doorstep without a disguise. Štefanko explains it masquerades as an adult player app that can't be downloaded from legitimate websites:

"Android/Twitoor is a backdoor capable of downloading other malware onto an infected device. It has been active for around one month. This malicious app, a variant of Android/Twitoor.A, can’t be found on any official Android app store – it probably spreads by SMS or via malicious URLs. It impersonates a porn player app or MMS application but without having their functionality."




Upon successful installation, Twitoor reveals its unique functionality: it listens to a predetermined Twitter account at regular intervals for commands, such as instructions to install banking malware/ransomware or switch to a new Twitter C&C account. Those messages are encrypted to help prevent security researchers and law enforcement from listening in.

The ESET researcher says these measures enhance the botnet's security and flexibility:


"These communication channels are hard to discover and even harder to block entirely. On the other hand, it’s extremely easy for the crooks to re-direct communications to another freshly created account."


Twitoor's handlers might have chosen Twitter for their botnet's communication because they know what can happen if a C&C server is exposed.

Back in April 2015, researchers at ESET registered a domain name to act as a C&C server for the Mumblehard Linux botnet. The attackers responded by eliminating all but one C&C server tied to a single IP address, a misstep which the researchers exploited to take over the IP address, sinkhole the server, and in essence shut down the botnet.


Statistics from Mumblehard sinkhole after the publication (Source: ESET)

Whenever a botnet goes offline, a drop in malware/ransomware infections is sure to follow. That's exactly what happened to Locky ransomware and the Dridex banking trojan after the Necurs botnet temporarily went quiet in the early-summer of 2016.

Twitoor is the first of its kind. As such, Štefanko believes botnet operators will look to it and other social media platforms for inspiration:


"In the future, we can expect that the bad guys will try to make use of Facebook statuses or deploy LinkedIn and other social networks."


Android users are urged to protect themselves against Twitoor and similar forms of malware by installing applications only from trusted developers found on the Google Play Store.
