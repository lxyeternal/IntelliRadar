The end goal of compromising Facebook accounts was distribution of deceptive ads for counterfeit goods and diet pills.

The operation

The company in question is ILikeAd Media International Company Ltd, represented by Chen Xiao Cong and Huang Tao, who Facebook says that are the authors of the malware behind the entire promo scheme.

One method was to use images of celebrities in their ads, also known as 'celeb bait,' to trick users into clicking on them.

A second practice is called 'cloaking' and consists in hiding from Facebook's systems the real destination of a link carried by the advertisement.

When clicked, the ad would take users to the real landing page while Facebook would be served a version that abides by the platforms Terms and Advertising Policies.

"Cloaking schemes are often sophisticated and well organized, making the individuals and organizations behind them difficult to identify and hold accountable," Facebook says.

This is the reason for the scarcity of legal actions against this sort of practice, the social media platform notes.

Facebook refunded the users that fell victim to these schemes and got their accounts used to run unauthorized ads and helped them secure their accounts.

An estimation of the losses, which would also be indicative of the success of the operation, is not provided.

Facebook pledges to continue its fight against this sort of malicious behavior that affects its platform and to enforce the terms of its agreement and policies.

Info-stealer goes after Ads Manager data

Recently, attackers have been observed pushing a fake PDF editor that steals Facebook and Amazon session cookies. The malware, detected as Socelars, is signed and security researcher MalwareHunterTeam saw it distributed through numerous websites.

An analysis from Vitali Kremez revealed that it was a new trojan that shared little with other malware of the same kind. The researcher told BleepingComputer that it could be an improved variant than the previous generation that is only starting to target ad and social media providers.

It is unclear if Socelars is connected to the company sued by Facebook today, but it sure fits the bill. Kremez told us that the trojan tries to steal Facebook session cookies from both Chrome and Firefox web browsers.

Cookies are then used to connect to multiple Facebook URLs. One of them accesses the 'account_billing' directory, which holds information that allows calling a Facebook Graph API and extract data from the user's Ads Manager settings.

The data collected this way includes session cookies, access tokens, account ids, advertising email address, associated pages, credit card info (number, expiration date), PayPal email, ad balances, spending limits, and more.
