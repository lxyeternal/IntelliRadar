
	   


A mother in Houston, Texas was horrified to learn hackers had compromised her home's security camera system and put up a live feed of her two daughters' bedroom online.

Jennifer, who has requested the media not include her last name, told ABC News she found out about the feed after her friend notified her of a post she had seen on Facebook.

The post originated from Shelby Ivie, a mother in Oregon. While looking at satellite images of Earth with her son, Ivie decided to download Live Camera Feed, a free app that allows users to view the footage of more than 50,000 CCTV and surveillance and security cameras in real time.

It was then that Ivie came across the feed of the daughters' bedroom.



She couldn't believe what she was seeing. As she told ABC News:


"I was in tears, thinking of the violation [Jennifer] must feel."


Determined to let the family know of the privacy intrusion, Ivie took a screenshot of the feed and posted it on news outlet websites and social media. In total, the image received 4,000 shares.

Upon learning of the hack, Jennifer hired a security consulting company to figure out what happened. It turns out one of her daughters accidentally opened the door to a group of hackers when she decided to play Minecraft on an unprotected server. It was child's play for the attackers to identify the IP address of the daughter's iPad. From there, they made their way to the modem and DVR system, which is connected to the home's security cameras.

Jennifer can't believe security cameras actually put her daughters in danger:


"We have security cameras to protect them. I feel like I've failed. ... People are watching my kids in their home, dressing, sleeping, playing."




Security cameras do help protect us, but as Jennifer found out the hard way, that's true only if we first make an effort to protect them. As a result of their generic IP addresses or public-facing websites, security cameras can easily be compromised by attackers armed with just a username and password.

With that in mind, people should always make sure to change the default settings on their security camera if and when they decide to purchase one.

But that's not the only way strangers could end up watching your children online. Actors of all levels of technical expertise use remote access trojans like BlackShades to "slave" victims' computers. Those types of attackers commonly take control of the victim's webcam and blackmail them with the threat of uploading sensitive webcam footage to the internet. To prevent that from happening, the actors commonly demand money or media of a sexual nature.

Law enforcement has gotten better in recent years at cracking down on those who look to infect people with RATs. (The stories of Konstantin Simeonov Kavrakov and Stefan Rigo immediately come to mind.) But it's up to users to keep themselves safe. They can do that by avoiding suspicious links and email attachments and, as in the case of Jennifer's daughters, playing online games only on protected servers.
