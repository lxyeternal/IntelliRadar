To prevent automated systems from creating accounts with their services, most websites rely on CAPTCHA-like mechanisms. This method presents a challenge to prove the human condition of the subscriber.

Google's reCAPTCHA offers the visually impaired the possibility solve the challenge by listening to an audio message and typing in what they hear.

In April 2017, a team of researchers from the Univesity of Maryland developed an attack method that bypassed the reCAPTCHA challenge with 85.15% accuracy in just 5.42 seconds.

Kevin Bock, Daven Patel, George Hughey, and Dave Levin created a tool called unCaptcha that can download the audio, segment it, and upload it to multiple speech-to-text services to convert the message; in the last step, the answer obtained this way was typed into the reCAPTCHA form to solve the challenge.



Google released a new version of the mechanism and the team managed to defeat the newly-implemented protections once again, using an updated version of the tool.

unCaptcha2 proved to be more efficient, with higher accuracy across all challenges. The same approach as before was used:

Navigate to Google's ReCaptcha Demo site
	Navigate to audio challenge for ReCaptcha
	Download audio challenge
	Submit audio challenge to Speech To Text
	Parse response and type answer
	Press submit and check if successful
Talking to BleepingComputer, George Hughey said that using Google's text-to-speech service, the tool recorded a 91% success rate over 600 trials.

"I have tested unCaptcha2 with Google's, Microsoft's, IBM's, and Wit.ai's speech-to-text services, and they were all successful," the researcher told us, mentioning that he did not have the exact accuracy percentage for the other services (the code to support them is available in his GitHub repo).

unCaptcha2 manages to trick Google's system by using a screen clicker to mimic human behavior on the webpage.



The four researchers in June 2018 demonstrated to Google how solving the challenges can still be automated. They decided to wait for six months to make their finding public, to give Google time to address the issues.

"The Recaptcha team is aware of this attack vector, and have confirmed they are okay with us releasing this code, despite its current success rate," the researchers say.

Google labeled the attack "Intended Behavior" and did not inform the researchers about plans to update the mechanism.

In late October, Google rolled the third version of reCaptcha, which performs a risk analysis after observing the behavior of website visitors. If the assessment determines human interaction, the user can move forward with a single click.
