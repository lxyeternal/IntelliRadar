Her announcement comes on the same day Yahoo filed its quarterly 10-K report with the SEC, where the company revealed that a third-party forensics firm discovered clues that over 32 million Yahoo accounts might have been accessed illegally using forged cookies in 2015 and 2016.

Yahoo says these account hacks are related to a data breach it disclosed last year.

In September 2016, Yahoo acknowledged a data breach that took place in 2014, which allowed an unknown hacker/group to steal personal details from more than 500 million users.

Yahoo admitted to a second breach in December 2016, this time saying the hack took place in 2013. For the second incident, Yahoo said hackers stole details for over 1 billion accounts.

Both Yahoo security incidents are today's top two biggest breaches of all time. The company blamed both hacks on a "state-sponsored actor," but never provided evidence to substantiate its claims.

Hackers accessed 32 million Yahoo accounts using forged cookies

According to another Yahoo SEC 10-K report filed in November 2016, Yahoo admitted that it knew of the 2014 data breach and kept quiet all these years.

The filing also revealed that Yahoo knew the attacker had accessed proprietary source code that allowed him to learn how to create forged cookies and access Yahoo accounts without the user's password.

Initially, Yahoo said the attacker used this technique to access only a few accounts. This may also be one of the reasons why Yahoo blamed to incident on a state-sponsored actor, believing these were targeted attacks carried out only against a handful of selected individuals.

In yesterday's latest SEC filing, Yahoo says that the number of Yahoo accounts compromised via forged cookies is bigger than the initial estimation, and is now at over 32 million.

An admission of guilt?

All cookie forging hacks are related to the 2014 security breach, disclosed in September 2016. A New York Times editorial revealed that security took a backseat at Yahoo during Mayer's tenure, a reason why its Chief Security Officer, Alex Stamos, left the company for a similar position at Facebook, right in the middle of the 2014 hack, after he and Mayer clashed several times. Yesterday's SEC filing gives credibility to NYT's report.

In late 2014, senior executives and relevant legal staff were aware that a state-sponsored actor had accessed certain user accounts by exploiting the Company’s account management tool. The Company took certain remedial actions, notifying 26 specifically targeted users and consulting with law enforcement. While significant additional security measures were implemented in response to those incidents, it appears certain senior executives did not properly comprehend or investigate, and therefore failed to act sufficiently upon, the full extent of knowledge known internally by the Company’s information security team. Specifically, as of December 2014, the information security team understood that the attacker had exfiltrated copies of user database backup files containing the personal data of Yahoo users but it is unclear whether and to what extent such evidence of exfiltration was effectively communicated and understood outside the information security team. However, the Independent Committee did not conclude that there was an intentional suppression of relevant information.
Nonetheless, the Committee found that the relevant legal team had sufficient information to warrant substantial further inquiry in 2014, and they did not sufficiently pursue it. As a result, the 2014 Security Incident was not properly investigated and analyzed at the time, and the Company was not adequately advised with respect to the legal and business risks associated with the 2014 Security Incident. The Independent Committee found that failures in communication, management, inquiry and internal reporting contributed to the lack of proper comprehension and handling of the 2014 Security Incident. The Independent Committee also found that the Audit and Finance Committee and the full Board were not adequately informed of the full severity, risks, and potential impacts of the 2014 Security Incident and related matters.

Mayer's gesture to pass down her bonus to employees may be a tacit apology and admission of guilt. Following the formal approval of Yahoo's sale to Verizon, Mayer will resign as Yahoo CEO.
