
	   


30% of companies from the automotive industry do not have their own cybersecurity program or team, nor use the services of an external organization to secure the software used in their products even though today's cars are mobile computers on wheels that use software every second of the day, 

When it comes to testing vulnerabilities roughly 63% percent of all automotive companies test less than half of software, hardware, and other technologies they develop.

These numbers were uncovered by an independent study conducted by Ponemon Institute and commissioned by Synopsys, Inc. and SAE International, which used a "sampling frame of 15,900 IT security practitioners and engineers in the automotive industry," while the final sample "consisted of 593 surveys" answered.

Automotive cybersecurity report conclusions

As further detailed in the report, "The security professionals surveyed for our report indicated that the typical automotive organization has only nine full-time employees in its product cybersecurity management program."

60 percent of all responders said that a lack of understanding/training on secure coding practices is the primary behind vulnerabilities in the automotive software/technology/components.

On the other hand, 55% cited accidental coding errors, 50% pointed at the lack of quality assurance and testing procedures, while 40% quote the use of insecure/outdated open source software components as the most common factors that lead to vulnerabilities in their technologies.

When asked about what were the technologies that pose the highest degree of cybersecurity risk, RF tech such as Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and hotspots were picked by 63% of respondents, while self-driving (autonomous) vehicles were chosen by 58% and telematics by 60%.

As further detailed in the study, "Seventy-three percent of respondents surveyed in our report say they are very concerned about the cybersecurity posture of automotive technologies supplied by third parties. However, only 44 percent of respondents say their organizations impose cybersecurity requirements for products provided by upstream suppliers."

Among the key findings of the report, the fact that automotive software security is not keeping up with the technology development pace within the auto industry is the most important.

Additionally, software developed as part of the automotive supply chain still presents a major risk because of a lack of cybersecurity assessment, while connected vehicles have unique security issues which could be limited or completely removed with proper security vulnerability analysis.



It's also important to add that while the numbers look grim, the security risks unveiled by this study aren't just theoretical.

Since software has been added to cars, security vulnerabilities have been found more and more often, with a large percent of them also vulnerable to exploitation by malicious actors.

For example, a Dutch cyber-security firm discovered in April 2018 that in-vehicle infotainment (IVI) systems used by some car models from the Volkswagen Group were vulnerable to remote hacking.

Also, during October 2017, an electronics designer discovered a flaw in the key fob system of several Subaru models, a security issue the automaker refused to patch when contacted and that could potentially be abused to hijack its customers' cars.

Mazda cars were also affected, with the Mazda MZD Connect infotainment system being easily hackable by plugging in a USB flash drive into the car's dashboard, a "feature" used by users to alter their cars' infotainment system by tweaking settings and installing new apps.

Tesla's electric cars were also found to be vulnerable during 2016, car thieves being able to hack and steal a Tesla by infecting the owner's phone with an Android malware strain.



Seeing that a driver's safety on the road is most of the time under the supervision of software running on the car's computing system, automakers should invest a lot more in secure coding practices and vulnerability testing, as early as possible in the production process.

This is especially important since the report says that "Eighty-four percent of the respondents to our survey have concerns that cybersecurity practices are not keeping pace with the ever-evolving security landscape."
