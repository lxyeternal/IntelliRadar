
	   


It's easy to set up a cybercriminal operation these days. But not all crooks are cut out for this game, some ending up not just infecting their own computers but also leaving identifying evidence on supporting infrastructure that is insecure and open to snooping.

Unskilled cybercriminals are common, but what changed over the years is their age, shifting from fully-grown individuals to early-teen children, making the description literal.

Various malware strains bundle in all the components required to get the 'business' going, including code for the command and control (C2) server for managing the operation.

Script-kiddies in the past

Security researchers find administration panels for malware operated by script-kiddies (criminals with little to no experience) on a daily basis. A few years back, they would look at a dozen C2 servers every day, MalwareHunterTeam told us.

In 2016, he tweeted about a skiddie that fell victim to the malware he was deploying to their victims. Proof of this was the screenshot the keylogger took of their desktop screen, found in the infection logs on the C2 server.



In reply to his tweet, TZ Security showed a Nigerian scammer caught by the camera of his own computer. The file was also discovered in the logs of the admin panel for the malware he used.



Script kiddies today

Today, these panels continue to be a frequent find. Since numerous youngsters figure themselves as online villains, they try to taste life on the other side of the law and set up small, low-cost cyber operations.

Those that don't know how to play the game end up infecting dozens of computers, even hundreds, at the same time failing at shielding themselves from their own malware.

MisterCh0c, an ethical hacker that looks at admin panels for malware on a daily basis, tweeted yesterday about an operator running the OrangeFox information stealer, which he deems to be an unsophisticated tool, yet a technical analysis on it is yet to emerge.

The researcher, who does this work pro bono, found the OrangeFox botnet panel while looking at another one for the PHP-based BlackNET botnet, available on GitHub and multiple hacker forums.

It turned out that the same operator was behind both these botnets and several more, one of them for the Arkei info-stealer, running on different servers.

Handling so many botnets may seem like the work of a cybercriminal mastermind, but not this time. A full-face pic in the logs shows a dead-eyed teen looking straight into the computer screen.



One of the panels MisterCh0c looked at showed over 180 infections, although this number may not be accurate because the operator deletes the logs regularly.

Getting access to these panels is sometimes a matter of luck, as the owners leave them open, thinking that no one would find it. Other times, reaching the log files and stats is possible due to vulnerabilities in the source code of the panel.

It's not like there weren't teen cybercriminals before, but with this example it seems like they're getting into the game at a younger age than before. 

Reporting illegal activity is useless sometimes

MalwareHunterTeam still finds time to keep up with the trends and bypasses the low-security fences wannabe malware operators have for their administration panels. Although he reports his findings and some of them are handled in minutes, while in many cases they fall on deaf ears.



Unfortunately, this appears to be a widespread problem. MisterCh0c told us that he also contacts authorities about these operations "but in my experience, they don't care and don't / can't do anything about it."

In mid-December 2018 the researcher reported to authorities a French cybercriminal (called 'la baguette') that was stealing from banks, yet their operations continue to be active even today.

Reporting the C2 panels to hosting providers is also not always a viable solution, either, because the crooks may use bullet-proof hosts - hosting companies that take no action against criminal activity on their servers.

Contacting the victims directly proved to be a bad idea for the researcher at least once. Warning the owner of a cryptocurrency wallet that their private key got stolen ended with threats from the victim.

With reporting not doing too good a job curbing the activity of skiddies, it should come as no surprise that some of them settle for this life outside the law.
