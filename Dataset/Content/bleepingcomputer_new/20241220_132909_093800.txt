To date, IoT devices are responsible for the two largest DDoS attacks ever recorded, against the website of journalist Brian Krebs (620 Gbps) and French ISP OVH (1.1 Tbps), but they're also responsible for the DDoS attack on Dyn, a managed DNS provider, which has brought down a large chunk of the Internet in the US and parts of Europe.

These are not dormant threats, but current-day problems. Just yesterday, a botnet of IoT devices had unabashedly attempted to bring down the Internet connection of Liberia, a small African country, by DDoSing its state-operated ISP.

If used correctly, IoT devices can be the perfect bots inside DDoS botnets. Malware such as Mirai, GafGyt, LuaBot, Kaiten, BillGates, Rex, and the more recent IRCTelnet, bounce around the Internet and infect devices, adding them to botnets, sometimes going over the 100,000 figure.

Botnet herders create their vast empires of hacked devices by brute-forcing Internet-exposed Telnet or SSH ports, but also by leveraging vulnerabilities in the control panel or the firmware of these devices.

Both problems, open ports and vulnerabilities, could be patched if users updated their device's firmware. Unfortunately, this is not as simple as it looks, as this requires some technical skills. Firmware patches can often fail and render the device useless, and some knowledge of CLI interfaces is also needed.

Despite the fact that many IoT devices are built on Linux-based operating systems, these devices have remained notoriously hard to update, despite the recent advancements in Linux distros.

Ubuntu Core 16 promises transactional updates for IoT devices

Ubuntu, who is one of the more user-friendly Linux variations, could be the answer. Canonical, who's been working on Ubuntu Core 16 since September, says that this version of their famous OS has been on purpose slimmed down to the necessary components needed to run on IoT devices, which have fewer physical resources at their disposal when compared to desktops and laptops.

But the features that made Ubuntu great were kept, including support for atomic and transactional updates.

An atomic update means a device vendor can update just a small library or component in the entire OS. A transactional update means if a user initiates an update procedure and something goes wrong, the old firmware will remain in place, and the device will continue to function.

These two features take the fear and pain out of firmware updates, which sometimes have left users looking at dead devices.

Snap packages are coming to IoT

Ubuntu Core 16 also supports a new feature called "snaps," which is a method of packaging Linux apps and components, in a way that ensures the app is securely confined, read-only, tamper-proof, and digitally signed.

Snaps will help IoT devices preserve the integrity of sensitive components, meaning malware will have a hard time modifying core libraries and processes for malicious behavior.

Since the OS itself and the Linux kernel are delivered as snap packages, the entire device upgrade process can be automated to the push of a button, in the device's backend.

Ubuntu Core 16 bringing OTAs to IoT

Even better, Ubuntu has worked with industry partners such as IBM and Dell to give manufacturers a way of delivering updates whenever the vendor detects a critical vulnerability or exploited zero-day. Think of it as the OTA (over-the-air) update system that Android devices never got.

With the number of IoT devices expected to reach 20 billion in the next decade, Ubuntu Core 16 is an attempt to correct the error that Google made when it released Android without a simple way to push updates to its end users.
