
	   


A new xHelper Trojan dropper variant capable of reinstalling itself after being removed or following factory resets has infected more than 45,000 Android devices over the last six months.

xHelper's creators mainly deliver it onto devices of targets from the U.S., India, and Russia in the form of a malicious app component with stealth capabilities that allow it to hide from the system's launcher.

Trojan droppers are malware strains used to deliver other more dangerous malware to already infected systems such as banking Trojans, clicker Trojans, and ransomware.

While the infection vector used by the threat actor behind the new xHelper variant is not yet known, Symantec's research team suspects that the app component that bundles the xHelper payloads is downloaded by a malicious system app that might come pre-installed on some smartphone brands.

The fact that "numerous users have been complaining on forums about the persistent presence of this malware on their devices, despite performing factory resets and manually uninstalling it," seems to further consolidate their hypothesis. — xHelper reports can be found on Reddit and Google Play's Help forums.

The number of devices infected with the xHelper Android malware grows each day, since "in the past month alone, there was an average of 131 devices infected each day, and an average of 2,400 devices persistently infected throughout the month," as Symantec's research team adds.

 



xHelper malicious activity

After successfully infecting a device, the xHelper Trojan dropper will start displaying random ad pop-ups and download other malicious apps.

The app that infects Android devices with the malware is hidden and cannot be launched manually by the user but it will auto-launch when "when the compromised device is connected to or disconnected from a power supply, the device is rebooted, or an app is installed or uninstalled."

After executing on the now compromised device, the malware will register as a foreground service to avoid getting automatically killed by the system when the device gets too low, and it will also restart the service just in case it ever dies or gets killed.

In the next step, it will connect to its command and control (C2) server using a communication channel encrypted using SSL certificate pinning, a technique commonly used to secure communication against man-in-the-middle (MiTM) attacks.


Triggering events

"Upon successful connection to the C&C server, additional payloads such as droppers, clickers, and rootkits, may be downloaded to the compromised device," the research team adds.

"We believe the pool of malware stored on the C&C server to be vast and varied in functionality, giving the attacker multiple options, including data theft or even complete takeover of the device."

Previously spotted by Malwarebytes Labs, continuously evolving

Symantec reports that they initially detected xHelper in March when the malware's code was relatively simple. However, the newest variants they spotted have been improved with anti-detection capabilities showing that it is continuously developed.

xHelper was also observed by Malwarebytes while slowly but steadily spreading to more and more Android devices, eventually managing to infect more  than 32,000 Android smartphones and tablets between May and August.

Malwarebytes also found that xHelper came in two distinct 'flavors' at the time: one that will also show some hints of its presence and another one that performs malicious tasks in full stealth mode.


Semi-stealth xHelper version

This comes as further proof that the malware is under active development given that the second variant detected by Malwarbytes' research team seems to be the natural progression to a more stealthy and harder to detect strain.

Symantec's research team recommends users to follow these tips to avoid getting infected with this persistent and almost impossible to get rid off malware:


• Keep your software up to date.
• Do not download apps from unfamiliar sites.
• Only install apps from trusted sources.
• Pay close attention to the permissions requested by apps.
• Make frequent backups of important data.


Malwarebytes Labs also recommends being cautious while browsing the web and carefully selecting the websites you visit on your Android device.
