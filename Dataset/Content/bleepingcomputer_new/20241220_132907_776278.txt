Spotted by Peruvian security researcher David Montenegro, the exploit kit — named Disdain — is available for rent on a daily, weekly, or monthly basis. The prices are $80, $500, and $1,400 respectively. The pricing points are smaller than fellow Nebula EK, which is priced at $100, $600, and $2,000.

To be a good exploit kit, Disdain has to provide "exploits" that renters can use to infect users. People who rent exploit kits redirect traffic to Disdain's homepage (known as a "landing page"), where the exploit kit scans the user's browser and attempts to use one of the exploits to install malware on the victim's computer.

Older exploit kits have a larger exploit collection because they've been in business for a longer time. Being in business for what appears to be a week, Disdain's arsenal is considerably smaller but contains quite a large number of newer exploits. Disdain's author has a bad reputation

The Disdain ad was first spotted last week. Currently, there is no malvertising campaign or botnet redirecting traffic to any Disdain "landing page," according to a security researcher who spoke with Bleeping Computer about Disdain but did not want to reveal his name.

One reason why we haven't seen any active campaign might be that Disdain's author — Cehceny — is currently banned and marked as a "ripper" (scammer) on at least one major underground hacking forum.



Below is the content of that forum topic, provided to Bleeping Computer by Radware security researcher Daniel Smith.



Exploit kit market in shambles

If criminal groups are willing to look past Cehceny's bad reputation, rent and deploy Disdain in malvertising campaigns, then Disdain will become one of the very few new exploits kits that has entered the market this year.

The exploit kit market has been on a downward spiral for the past 16 months. During this period several major exploit kits have shut down, such as Angler, Nuclear, Neutrino, Sundown, Sweet Orange, and Fiesta.

On the other hand, very few new players have surfaced, and most are run by inexperienced crews. This year, we've seen new players such as Sundown-Pirate, and a few other EKs that have survived only a few months.

We've previously talked extensively about the decline of exploit kits in a previous article. The main reasons that exploit kits are fading is because browsers are getting harder to hack and have fewer vulnerabilities, making it economically unfeasible to run an exploit kit for its creators.

Currently, the exploit kit market includes players such as RIG, Rig-V, Terror, Magnitude, Kaixin, and Nebula, according to Execute Malware.

This lack of activity from the exploit kit market explains why Symantec saw spam levels surge to its highest level in the past two years, since March 2015.

UPDATE [August 17, 07:50 ET]: Security firm Trend Micro has detected the Disdain kit in active malvertising campaigns. Researchers also noted that Disdain shares code with the older Terror exploit kit.
