QakBot, also known as Qbot or PinkSlip, is a banking trojan that was first spotted in 2007. Despite being deployed in malware campaigns very rarely, QakBot is one of the most advanced banking trojans on the market today, with many features rivaling the more famous Dridex, Ramnit, or Gozi trojans.

To avoid over-exposing their operations, QakBot's authors avoid spreading their banking trojan via mundane spam targeting average Joes.

During the past few years, researchers have spotted only a few QakBot campaigns. There was one in October 2014, then one in April 2016. Recently, in mid-May, researchers spotted another wave of QakBot attacks.

QakBot adds support for self-mutating mechanism

With this new wave of attacks, QakBot received new features. The most important of these is support for a polymorphism mechanism that allows the malware to self-mutate in transit, as it moves inside a company's network.

This adds to an already impresive arsenal. A review of QakBot's most important features reveals that this banking trojan is the work of some very talented developers:

⮡  Qakbot features worm capabilities to self-replicate through shared drives and removable media.
⮡  Uses a webinjection mechanism to steal banking credentials.
⮡  Modular architecture with modules for implementing backdoor features, SOCKS proxy, information-stealing features, etc.
⮡  Can disable security products running on an endpoint (if it gains admin privileges).
⮡  QakBot's dropper uses a delayed execution of 10-15 minutes to elude sandbox environments.
⮡  After deployment, the dropper corrupts its original file to avoid detection and analysis.
⮡  QakBot uses both hardcoded C&C servers and a DGA for dynamically generated C&C domain names
⮡  Malware uses both registry key hacks and scheduled tasks to remain on infected machines

QakBot features an aggressive brute-forcing module

Besides the self-mutation system detected by Cylance researchers during the last wave of QakBot attacks, IBM's X-Force team has also spotted an older feature that has been improved.

This is a secondary self-spreading method that relies on brute-forcing other endpoints interconnected by the same domain controller.

For this, QakBot uses a list of hardcoded credentials to launch dictionary attacks on nearby endpoints, hoping to gain access to new machines.

The entire process is a little bit more complex than this simplistic explanation, but in most cases, the multitude of failed authentication attempts triggers the domain controller to lock out the machine where all these failed logins are originating. This results in the infected machine getting locked out of its AD domain.

System administrators at high-value targets that have to deal with machines mysteriously getting locked out of the company's domain should look into scanning the system for QakBot IOCs.

Based on IBM telemetry, QakBot is currently ranked tenth in the Banking Trojan Top 10, with a "market share" of 2%.
