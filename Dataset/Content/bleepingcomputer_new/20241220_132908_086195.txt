
	   


The U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) says that a settlement has been reached with Retina-X Studios, LLC (Retina-X), the company behind three 'stalkerware' mobile applications, that bars it from selling the apps unless they are used for legitimate purposes.

Stalkerware apps are designed to allow third-parties to monitor one's mobile device without the user's knowledge and, in some cases, to collect sensitive user information like location and online activity that later could be used for blackmail and various other malicious purposes.

"This is our first action against a so-called ‘stalking app," said  Director of the FTC’s Bureau of Consumer Protection Andrew Smith.

"Under these circumstances, we will seek to hold app developers accountable for designing and marketing a dangerous product."



Hacked stalkerware

Retina-X previously sold the MobileSpy app designed to monitor children and employees, and it was also behind two other 'stalkerware' apps called PhoneSheriff and TeenShield, also targeted at monitoring children's mobile devices.

"MobileSpy’s premium version also permitted monitoring consumers, from a remote online dashboard, to view the monitored mobile device’s screen in real time," says the Retina-X complaint. [PDF]

While the apps were a security risk on their own seeing that they often required the devices to be jailbroken before installing — exposing them to security vulnerabilities and, at times, voiding warranties — and they allowed the customers to remove their icons to operate without the users' knowledge, "even for legitimate users, the company failed to keep data – including children’s information – confidential and safe."

The developer stopped selling the apps in 2018 after its cloud storage was breached twice using unencrypted account credentials in February 2017 and with the help of 'obfuscated' credentials in February 2018.

The hacker managed to harvest and exfiltrate data collected using the PhoneSheriff and TeenShield apps, "including login usernames, encrypted login passwords, text messages, GPS locations, contacts, and photos."

"The company and Johns did not learn about the first intrusion until April 2017 when they were contacted by a journalist, who was tipped off by the hacker," the FTC press release says.

Stalking apps banned, no longer sold

Before Retina-X effectively stopped marketing and selling the three stalking apps, it managed to get customers to pay for 15,000 subscriptions (5,700+ for MobileSpy, 4,600+ for PhoneSheriff, and over 5,000 for TeenShield) in total for all three apps.

"MobileSpy, PhoneSheriff and TeenShield have not been available for purchase since April 2018. However, Respondents’ websites for each of these monitoring products and services remain online," also states the complaint.

"The settlement prohibits Retina-X and Johns from promoting, selling, or distributing any monitoring app that requires users to circumvent a device’s security protections to install it, absent reasonable steps to ensure that the app is being used for legitimate purposes," states the press release.

FTC's settlement orders Retina-X and its owner, James N. Johns, Jr., to require their monitoring apps to always be used for legitimate purposes and with the written consent of the users that will be monitored.

Additionally, they must destroy all the data their customers have already collected using their stalking apps and monitoring services.

Also as part of the settlement, the company "must obtain third-party assessments of their information security program every two years," and also "must require purchasers to state that they will only use the app to monitor a child or an employee, or another adult who has provided written consent."
