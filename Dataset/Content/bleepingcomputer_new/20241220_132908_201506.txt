
	   


For the past two years, the source code for the Carbanak banking malware has been sitting on VirusTotal scanning platform, available for any researcher that recognized it.

The malware strain serves mainly remote access purposes but it can also record videos of the computer screen and send them to the attacker.

Developed and used by Russian-speaking individuals collectively known as the FIN7 group (also known as Anunak, Carbanak, and Cobalt, after the malware they used), the backdoor was used in big-money heists, targeting financial organizations to steal directly from them. Over the past few years, however, FIN7 expanded its target list to other sectors (hospitality, travel, energy, travel, education, retail, government, telecommunication), FireEye said last year.

Researchers at FireEye found it packed into two RAR archives (kb3r1p.rar and apwmie.rar). Uncompressed, one weighs about 15MB and has 879 files, and the other is about 6MB with 24 files. The files are also available through the VirusBay service, here and here.

FIN7 used Carbanak between 2014 and 2016. The group is responsible for attacking more than 100 banks all over the world and stealing well over $1 billion through their cash transfer systems. On April 19, 2017, Carbanak's source code was uploaded to VirusTotal, from Russia.



Since Nick Carr found the two archives in 2017, FireEye researchers spent 229 hours analyzing Carbanak's binaries and source code. Including work before finding the code, the researchers spent 469 hours on Carbanak; this effort led to unprecedented insight into the malware and its functionality, and enabled learning about plugins that had not been seen before.



Challenges ahoy

"Having source code sounds like cheat-mode for malware analysis. Indeed, source code contains much information that is lost through the compilation and linking process," says Michael Bailey, one of the researchers that analyzed the source code.

Malware analysts are accustomed to working with binary files, the version that is built to be understood by computers. The source code of an application is its human-readable version and offers much more information that is lost during the compilation process.

However, analyzing Carbanak's source code was not without challenges. The language barrier between its writers and FireEye's researchers was one of the obstacles, as Bailey explains:

"Reading the source code entailed two steps: displaying the files in the correct encoding, and learning enough Russian to be dangerous."



"I spent several hours on Russian language learning websites to study the pronunciation of Cyrillic characters and Russian words. Then, I looked up the top 600+ words and created a small dictionary," the researcher added.

From this effort resulted in graphical interfaces for Carbanak tools that had the commands translated to English. This way, non-Russian speaking researchers could take a look at the malware's functionality.



Another challenge in the analysis was the complexity of the code, which often had researchers jump to different sections in order to understand the route for processing a command.

"Analysis required bouncing around between almost 20 functions in 5 files, often backtracking to recover information about function pointers and parameters that were passed in from as many as 18 layers back," Bailey reveals.

Although FIN7's leader was arrested in Q1 2018, the group is still active and moved to a different malware strain that relies on Cobalt Strike,  legitimate tool for penetration testing, to build their utilities.

On August 13, 2018, Arbor Networks saw a phishing campaign that targeted banks in Russia and Romania. The evidence pointed to a FIN7 attack.

More recent activity was recorded in October 2018, when the group started to use a new version of the ThreadKit macro delivery tool, whose final payload was CobInt malware used by Cobalt group.
