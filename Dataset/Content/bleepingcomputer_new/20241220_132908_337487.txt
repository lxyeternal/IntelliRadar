
	   


According to a speculative cyber risk scenario prepared by Cambridge University for risk management purposes, a ransomware strain that would manage to impact more than 600,000 businesses worldwide within 24 hours would potentially lead to damages of billions not covered by insurers.

First of all, it is important to understand that although the numbers look very scary, this type of an attack is practically impossible to pull off at the moment when taking into consideration the current capabilities of malware, anti-malware, and current IT ecosystems.

To illustrate this point, in two of the theoretical scenarios used to create the report the malware used in the speculative global scale ransomware attack is capable of infecting machines running two operating systems, with a currently impossible to imagine "potential attack surface of 97.3% devices worldwide."

Insurance firms refusing to cover ransomware attack losses

Although the report "identifies opportunities for insurers to expand their business in insurance classes associated with ransomware attacks," quite recent events show that, in some circumstances, insurers have refused to cover the losses generated by ransomware attacks.

In the case of Mondelez for example, according to Bloomberg, the company "claimed $100 million on its insurance policy because it believed the permanent damage to 1,700 of its servers and 24,000 laptops, inflicted by NotPetya. [..] In June 2018, Zurich countered that NotPetya fell under an exclusion in the policy covering 'hostile or warlike action in time of peace or war,' which meant the insurer didn't have to make good on the claim."

Furthermore, seeing that this study was co-produced by insurance and reinsurance organizations and sellers, it is important to also note that there is a benefit to be gained from a theoretical report like this that would make businesses want to buy specialized cyber insurance.

The theoretical Bashe malware attack

With that said, the "Bashe attack: Global infection by contagious malware" sponsored report was produced by Nanyang Technological University's Cyber Risk Management (CyRiM) project in collaboration with Lloyd's, Aon Centre for Innovation and Analytics, MSIG, and SCOR TransRe.

The report "explores how a ransomware attack might take place and what the impacts would be on governments, businesses, and the insurance sector."

According to the "hypothetical scenario developed as a stress test for risk management purposes" presented in the report, the speculative global cyber attack could lead to economic damages of at least $85 billion in the least severe scenario and up to $193 billion in the worst possible case.


Global ransomware attack unfolding scenario

The Lloyd’s press release through which the report was published, says that:


In the report’s scenario, the attack is launched through an infected email, which once opened is forwarded to all contacts and within 24 hours encrypts all data on 30 million devices worldwide. Companies of all sizes would be forced to pay a ransom to decrypt their data or to replace their infected devices. The report, [..] shows a ransomware attack on this scale would cause substantial economic damage to a wide range of business sectors through reduced productivity and consumption, IT clean-up costs, ransom payments and supply chain disruption.


One of the hypothetical infection scenarios taken into consideration by the report estimates that the most affected industries would be retail and healthcare with $25 billion in damages for each of them while damages for manufacturing companies will reach $25 billion.

The US could lose around $89 billion in total over all industries, followed by Europe with roughly $76 billion and Asia with around $19 billion, while the rest of the world would be hit with $9 billion in damages.

As insurance company Lloyd’s states, 86% out of the total economic costs of such a high scale cyber attack would be uninsured, effectively leaving approximately $166 billion of damages not covered.


Economic losses according to three possible scenarios

As per the speculative scenario presented in the report, the malware would be disseminated using phishing campaigns via a dropper which would download a self-propagating worm strain with ransomware capabilities.

To propagate to other networks or through the network it just infected, the malware would have to forward the original malicious email to all contacts it finds on the computers it manages to compromise, efficiently spreading itself through the entire organizations as well as to all other connected businesses.

The payload dropped on compromised machines by the malware strain used in the scenario would be the deciding factor behind the amount of data lost during the simulated cyber attacks, with the least severe disruption being provoked by DDoS, Credential Stealer, and Bot/Botnet payloads to the most affected devices being infected with ransomware and wiper malware.



Lloyd's says that a global scale ransomware attack that would successfully impact hundreds of thousands of companies, "claims would be made for business interruption, contingent business interruption, cyber extortion, incident response costs, personal cyber liability," with the highest possible amount of estimated damages paid by insurers reaching $27 billion. 

To conclude, Dr. Andrew Coburn, Chief Scientist at the Cambridge Centre for Risk Studies, stated that:


The scenario we have prepared with Lloyd’s, CyRiM and other contributors highlights the potential for loss that can occur from contagious malware attacks. [..] This report is intended to deepen the understanding of cyber risk liability and aggregation risk in the portfolios of insurers.


Security researchers are skeptical

Some security researchers are skeptical of this report. For example, in a tweet by security expert Dr. Vesselin Bontchev, it is pointed out that one of these scenarios is not even possible and that some of the statistics are made up.


One of their "scenarios" involves a dual-platform ransomware that infects 97.3% of all devices world-wide.
Dudes, 97.3% of all devices world-wide cannot be brought up on-line simultaneously, let alone infected.
Also, 97.3% of all statistics are made up.
— Vess (@VessOnSecurity) January 29, 2019
