
	   


Alexander Tverdokhlebov, 29, a former Russian national, now a US citizen living in Los Angeles, was sentenced to nine years in prison for operating a banking trojan botnet, and stealing money from victims' bank accounts.

According to court documents, Tverdokhlebov started his cybercrime lifestyle in 2008 when he became active on several Russian-speaking underground hacking forums.

US authorities say Tverdokhlebov became a respected and very well connected member of these communities and used his new-found friends to exchange hacking tools, illegal services, and stolen personal and financial information.

Besides buying and selling stolen data, Tverdokhlebov also created his own malware that he used to steal payment card and other sensitive financial information from compromised computers. US investigators say that between 2009 and 2013, Tverdokhlebov operated a botnet of nearly 500,000 infected PCs, from where he stole over 40,000 payment card numbers.

The hacker didn't only collect and sell stolen card numbers but also used so-called "money mule" services to cash out stolen funds from hijacked bank accounts.

Tverdokhlebov sent money from hacked bank accounts to the accounts of money mules, who used the funds to purchase expensive products, which they later resold for smaller prices and gave Tverdokhlebov a cut. In addition, money mules would also use fake payment cards to withdraw money from ATMs, also giving Tverdokhlebov a cut.

Tverdokhlebov also convinced two Russian students visiting the US to open bank accounts in their names so he could use these bank accounts as proxies in his money laundering operations.

Tverdokhlebov arrested based on clues he left in ICQ chats

The hacker ran his business undisturbed for years, until February 2017, when authorities finally managed to discover his real-world identity and arrest him.

According to court documents, investigators tracked down Tverdokhlebov because of clues he left in ICQ conversations with Vadim Polyakov, a 32-year-old Russian hacker who pleaded guilty last year to a StubHub hacking scheme.

US investigators believe that during his prodigious hacking career, Tverdokhlebov caused estimated damages ranging between $9.5 and $25 million.

Hacker had hidden stashes of money

When authorities arrested Tverdokhlebov in February, they found that the hacker had four keys to four safe-deposit boxes — three in Los Angeles and one in Las Vegas. In the safe-deposit boxes, Secret Service agents discovered $272,000 in $100 bills.

Because authorities suspected the hacker might have had other stashes of cash hidden around the US, a judge declined to release him on bail.

Tverdokhlebov pleaded guilty on March 31 and was sentenced last Friday in a Virginia court. Besides the prison sentence, the hacker will also serve three years of supervised release that include monitoring of his computer use.
