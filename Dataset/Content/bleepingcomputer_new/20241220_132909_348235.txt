Coined by researchers at RiskIQ, the name Magecart refers to groups that scrape card data via malicious JavaScript code that loads on checkout pages. In late February, the company had discovered 12 distinct Magecart groups.

Group-IB, a security outfit specialized in detecting and preventing cyber attacks, refers to the malicious code planted on victim websites as JS-Sniffers and splits them into two categories: universal and built for a specific content management (CMS) or payment system.

In a report shared with BleepingComputer, the company analyzed 15 of the 38 JS-Sniffer families discovered. The researchers found that 2,440 websites had been infected with a JS-Sniffer. An estimated 1.5 million unique visitors land on those sites on a daily basis; this does not reflect the number of victims, though.



According to the researchers, at least eight of the families have not been analyzed before.

Some of them work can steal card data from different payment systems and do not need per-website adjustments. G-Analytics and WebRank collect all information indicated in a hardcoded list of HTML elements and selection of the data occurs on the server side, via an administrative panel.

Other families, like PreMage, MagentoName, FakeCDN, Qoogle, GetBilling, ImageID, and Post Eval target e-commerce websites using Magento, Shopify, and OpenCart CMS, or the WooCommerce plugin for WordPress.

"These JS-sniffers use a hardcoded list of names that correspond to the fields in a payment form. The list of field names is used to search for the victim’s payment information," the report details.

Tricks from the advanced ones

Some developers manage to keep their code under the radar for a period by using a unique code for each infection (CoffeMokko). In the case of ImageID and ReactGet, the code is active only when the buyer completes the transaction, which allows the malicious script to bypass most detection systems.

One of the sniffers widely used against online stores, ImageID can also disguise as legitimate services like Google Analytics and jQuery, a characteristic observed with the G-Analytics JS-Sniffer family, too.



Another family that indicates a level of sophistication is MagentoName, which targets multiple websites at a time and is capable to eliminate competitors' code.

"More than half of infected websites discovered by Group-IB researchers were affected by MagentoName family," Dmitry Volkov, CTO and Head of Threat Intelligence at Group-IB, told BleepingComputer.

Furthermore, MagentoName can remove other sniffers that had already infected its target.

Scripts in the WebRank family resort to a nasty trick to steal card information. Volkov says that the threat actor basically steals from the competition by intercepting payment data and sending it to its own gate.

WebRank operators used this strategy against the MagentoName JS-Sniffer by gaining access to its host. They modified the source code so that the resulting MagentoName script included WebRank's code, so the collected data siphoned to the latter's infrastructure.

Compromising the targets

To plant their code on victim websites, cybercriminals may exploit vulnerabilities in online store platforms (MagentoName targets older versions of Magento CMS); or steal the login credentials for the admin panel.

A third method is supply-chain attacks, where the original resource from a third-party service is infected. This method spreads the JS-Sniffer to multiple websites in one shot.

The researchers explain that "by hacking into the web analytics system, threat actors injected the JS-sniffer code into the web analytics script. This script, which is loaded by a large number of sites, loaded a bank card JS-sniffer along with itself."

MagentoName seems to be responsible for the largest number of victims, Group-IB says, as more than half of the comprmomised websites discovered by the researchers were infected with a JS-Sniffer from this family.



JS-Sniffers come at affordable prices

During their investigation, the Russian researchers saw less than ten different sources offering on underground forums to sell or rent JS-Sniffer code. The price ranges between $250 and $5,000.

Volkov told us that some underground services propose partnerships, where "the customer provides access to the compromised online store and receives a share [80%] of the profits."

The developer of the JS-Sniffer takes 20% of the profit and offers hosting servers, technical support, and an administrative panel the customer can use to parse the stolen details.

Even the most expensive of the offers may be attractive for some criminals groups. Statistics from Group-IB show that some websites infected by these malicious scripts have more that 150,000 daily visitors. If a fraction of them make a purchase every day, the potential monetization becomes attractive. Typically, card data sells between $1 and $5, but there is also the possibility of using it to purchase goods and resell them at lower price.
