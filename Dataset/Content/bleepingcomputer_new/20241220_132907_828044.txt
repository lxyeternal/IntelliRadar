
	   


A new sample of Shamoon disk-wiping malware was uploaded from France recently to the VirusTotal scanning platform. It tries to pass as a system optimization tool from Chinese technology company Baidu.

This new Shamoon variant was uploaded on December 23, 2018, and is signed with a digital certificate from Baidu, issued on March 25, 2015. The signature is no longer valid, as it expired on March 26, 2016.

The targets attacked by the threat actor behind this malware are typically oil and gas companies in the Middle East region. The latest Shamoon samples hit companies in the same area as well as Europe, deleting files on infected systems and making the machines unbootable.

Malware analysts also found that the malware was packed using version 4 of the commercial packing tool Enigma Protector, which had its major release in March 2014; further iterations of the branch followed until February 2015. Enigma Protector is currently at revision 6.40.

Research from Anomali Labs shows more attempts to disguise the malware as legitimate software. For instance, adversary gave the internal file name "Baidu PC Faster," and uses "Baidu WiFi Hotspot Setup" in its description.



These superficial modifications were also observed in an earlier Shamoon sample, which was packed using the UPX (Ultimate Packer for eXecutable) and used "VMWare Workstation" in its file description.

The researchers assess that this sample was likely "compiled based on the second version of the codebase," due to the many similarities with Shamoon v2, spotted between 2016 and 2017 in attacks against private organizations in the Middle East.

One indication of this is the presence of an image resource that depicts a burning US Dollar called 'Grant'; another resource included is named '101,' and was spotted in a sample analyzed by McAfee earlier this month. Previous versions of Shamoon, also referred to as Disttrack, contained images of a burning American flag and of a Syrian refugee child.



APT33 group likely involved

"At this time, Anomali Labs has not confirmed that this sample has been used to target victims in the wild. However, historic Shamoon 2 attacks occurred in November 2016 and late January 2017. The possibility for targeted attacks occurring during western holidays exists," say the researchers.

Anomali Labs cannot confirm if the actor behind this sample is the same as the attacker that struck oil companies in the past. The discoveries, however, suggest that the newly found Shamoon may be an old sample modified by a threat actor, not necessarily its original author.

In a blog post last week, McAfee says that after analyzing three Shamoon samples seen this year they reached the conclusion that the attacker is likely the Iranian hacker group known in the infosec space as APT33, or a threat actor trying to look like APT33.

The technical details from McAfee reveal similarities to past samples as well as some differences. For instance, Shamoon v3 uses a new wiper called Filerase, spotted by Symantec first.

The purpose of Filerase is to delete data on infected machines, while the malware's own wiping mechanism for the master boot record (MBR) renders the computer unbootable.

This tactic ensures that the data on the hard disk cannot be retrieved by placing the storage unit in a new, working machine.
