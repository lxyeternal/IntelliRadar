A group of at least two hackers has tried to extort a ransom payment from the Lloyds Banking Group according to a copy of an email the hackers sent to a high-ranking executive.

The extortion attempt took place on January 11, the first day some Lloyds Bank customers experienced short-lived problems with accessing their online banking portals.

Customers continued to report brief outages in the following two days. On the third day, on Friday, January 13, Bleeping Computer received two separate tips, via email and Twitter, from two hackers that appeared to know each other.

Hackers attempted to extort high-ranking bank executive

Hacker #1 sent Bleeping Computer a link to a PasteBin page that contained a copy of an email the group allegedly sent to a high-ranking Lloyds Bank manager.

The hackers were asking for 100 Bitcoin (£75,000 / $94,000). "Once paid, the services will be back online, you will get a list of flaws related to both services, along with our disappearance," the email reads.


Ransom email uploaded on PasteBin (Bitcoin address hidden at hacker's request)

A second hacker reached out via Twitter a few hours later and was surprised to find out that his colleague already shared the PasteBin link, confirming they knew each other.

Multiple sources: Lloyds hit with DDoS attack

Hacker #2 proceeded to provide a demo that allegedly showed they were behind the Lloyds Bank outages.

The demo was specific with how hackers demonstrate they are behind DDoS attacks. Hacker #2 asked your reporter and other journalists to access Lloyds Bank online portals before his attack, to prove the service was running, and during his attack, to show that he was the one causing the issues.

Reports published today by Bloomberg and Financial Times, citing inside sources, also claim Lloyds Bank had been the target of DDoS attacks during the same time period. A third report, from Motherboard, who was also asked to participate in the hacker's demo, confirms the Lloyds banking portals had gone down during the hacker's demonstration.

During the same timeframe, Hacker #2, using a newly registered and now-dormant account, had been busy announcing his attacks on Twitter.









Lloyds Bank hasn't yet pinned the blame on DDoS attacks

In a statement provided by Lloyds Bank today to Bleeping Computer via email, a spokesperson detailed what happened that week, but declined to comment on the nature of the downtime.


We experienced intermittent service issues with internet banking between Wednesday morning and Friday afternoon the week before last and are sorry for any inconvenience caused. We had a normal service in place for the vast majority of this period and only a small number of customers experienced problems. In most cases if customers attempted another log in they were able to access their accounts. We will not speculate on the cause of these intermittent issues.


An email to the high-ranking Lloyds Bank executive was not returned at the time of publishing.

In May 2016, Lloyds Bank was happily announcing that cyber-attacks against its infrastructure had gone down between 80% and 90%, as hackers were shifting their gaze towards new industry verticals.

At the time of writing, the Bitcoin adress included in the ransom note has a zero balance, with zero transactions.
