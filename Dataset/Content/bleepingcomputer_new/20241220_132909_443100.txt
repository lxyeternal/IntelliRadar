A report published today by cybersecurity company RiskIQ reveals that cases of scammers leaving their servers wide open are far from being rare, allowing researchers access to all the content on the server.

This helps with monitoring the scammer's activity and with collecting information for identifying indicators of compromise used for spotting and preventing navigation to risky URLs.

Visiting the index of a domain used for running fake survey campaigns, the researchers were able to see how long the host server was used for this type of activity.

In one case, they noticed that the server hosted multiple domains and the fraudulent activity began around September 18, 2017.



Details about the size of the operation were also gathered this way. One of the domains, for instance, (bootstraplugin[.]com) was associated with hundreds of other domains sustaining the fraudulent operation. Its registration date is January 17, 2016, which is also the start point of the scam campaigns monitored by RiskIQ.

RiskIQ threat researcher Yonathan Klijnsma says that his company has kept an eye on a fake survey campaign currently running on YouTube since 2016, although news outlets started publicizing it only recently.

The promise of a gift from a popular influencer

YouTube, like other platforms, allows its users to associate their name, which is displayed publicly in messages and posts, with a different account name.

However, when sending messages to other users, either publicly or privately, the receiver sees only the username and the avatar, not the account name that sent it.

Scammers take advantage of this discrepancy to give their messages legitimacy and trick victims into clicking on their links. By creating profiles with avatars of popular YouTubers, they increase the success rate of the scam.


message from spoofed YouTube account

"Notice how YouTube’s messaging system also displays the username—in this case, the impersonated name—below the message, further enhancing the credibility of the scam," Klijnsma explains.

He adds that scammers can choose between a large number of top YouTube accounts and they can impersonate content creators at scale.



The researcher describes a classic pattern that was observed in the past with survey scams on Facebook: after clicking on the link, users find themselves in a merry-go-round of shortlinks enticing them to complete surveys in return for personal information and the promise of an expensive prize, like an iPhone.

To make their activity believable, scammers often impersonate web pages of popular companies. If an iPhone is presented as a prize, they will show a fake Apple page.


fake Apple page

"Clicking “Get it Now” takes the visitor through a “selection process” that requires the visitor to provide their name, address, country, and email address. After a fake progress bar “checks” this information, the victim is presented with a message that they were selected as a winner," Klijnsma details.

Of course, getting such a desirable prize does not come without jumping through more hoops; a set of surveys are proposed to the user as a part of a process that verifies they are not a bot.



This is actually the purpose of the entire operation as the crooks get paid for filling out the surveys. By tricking a large number of users to do it for them, the criminals increase their revenue.

The goal here is to collect user personal information as this is what the crooks are actually selling. And since the volume of users falling for the con is no joke, the money is enough to allow scammers to rake in a profit and start other campaigns.

According to statistics from shortlink service Bitly, some links can get tens of thousands of clicks.



This type of fraud is unlikely to dwindle into extinction as long as there is the slightest chance to dupe users still exists. By running massive campaigns, crooks can pull in sufficient profit to encourage their activities.
