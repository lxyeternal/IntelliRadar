
	   


Instagram users are being targeted by a new phishing campaign that baits them into giving away their credentials using fake copyright infringement alerts which implants a feeling of urgency designed to lower the users' guard.

The phishing e-mails distributed as part of this campaign use fake account suspension messages supposedly triggered by copyright notice and asking them to fill out a 'Copyright Objection Form' within 24 hours.

Crooks use phishing to trick their targets into handing over sensitive info via fraudulent websites they control with the help of various social engineering techniques.

"No one wants to get locked out of their social media account, even temporarily, over an unresolved argument about an image," says Sophos' Paul Ducklin who analyzed these phishing attacks.

"As a result, the temptation to click the link on the email is high – especially if you know that the ‘dispute’ is bogus or easily resolved, perhaps because you think you can quickly prove that you took the photos yourself."

The phishing messages are designed to look as close as possible to what official Instagram messages would look like to avoid raising suspicions before the potential victims click the "Copyright Objection Form" button and get redirected to the phishing landing page.


Phishing email, interstitial, and landing page

However, before landing on the phishing page, the targets will first see an interstitial designed to increase the feeling of urgency, stating that their accounts "will be deactivated within 48 hours, unless you provide feedback."

This should provide the targeted users with at least some sort of hint that something is 'phishy' given that the phishing email says that the accounts will get suspended within 24 hours.

The attackers use an insta­gram.copy­right­infringement­appeal.[phishingdomain].cf subdomain (.CF being the country code top-level domain for the Central African Republic) for both the interstitial and the phishing landing page which adds to the illusion that the domain is an Instagram one on mobile web browsers.

Also, the two pages are secured with a valid HTTPS certificate issued by Let's Encrypt and display green padlocks to further alleviate the users' doubts that the page they're on is the real deal. This stands to show that even if you see a green padlock announcing that the connection is secured, checking if the domain is a legitimate one is also a must.

The phishing landing page also displays an age confirmation form probably used to add even more legitimacy to the attack.

Once the targets enter their username and password and hit the 'Submit' button at the bottom of the phishing page, the credentials will immediately be uploaded to an attacker-controlled storage server reachable via the same .cf domain.


Decoy pages displayed after exfiltration

Another attempt to divert the victims' attention and avoid raising suspicion is made by displaying a loading page after the 'Submit' button is clicked, as well as decoy page saying that the copyright objection has been submitted. The victims are also told to wait 24 hours to be contacted by Instagram via email.

If the victims fall for this last trick, this would allow the attackers to take control of their accounts undisturbed, without having to fast forward through their account takeover procedure.

Last but not least, the victims will be automatically redirected to the official Instagram login page as a last effort to maintain the illusion that everything is fine and the copyright objection form was an authentic Instagram form.

What should you do after being hacked or phished

Some Instagram users are bound to fall for these types of scams given that phishers come back with new attacks almost every month.

Last month, for instance, Instagram users were targeted by a phishing campaign using login attempt warnings coupled with what looked like two-factor authentication (2FA) codes to make the attack more believable.

In April, two series of Instagram phishing attacks dubbed The HotList and The Nasty List were making their rounds on the social network attempting to steal login credentials and spreading around via previously hacked accounts that sent messages to their followers.

If you had your Instagram credentials stolen or had your account hacked but you can still access your account, you should first check if your correct phone number and email address are still associated with the account.

To do this you have to go to your profile, click on Edit Profile, then scroll to the bottom to view the current email address and phone number. If they've been changed by the attackers, try to enter your correct info. After this, you should also change your account's password by following these instructions provided by Instagram.

Changing the password will automatically log off all devices currently logged into your account, making it possible for you to log back in to regain full control of your Instagram account.

Below you can find Instagram's instructions on what to do if you're still able to log into your account:

• Change your password or send yourself a password reset email
• Revoke access to any suspicious third-party apps
• Turn on two-factor authentication for additional security

However, if you completely lost access to your account, you can use these instructions to report the incident to Instagram's security. Instagram will reinstate it after confirming your identity via a photo or "the email address or phone number you signed up with and the type of device you used at the time of sign up."
