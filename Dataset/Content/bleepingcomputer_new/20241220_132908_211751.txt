
	   


A spear-phishing campaign spanning more than five months has been targeting roughly 17 U.S. utility providers between April 5 and August 29 according to research from Proofpoint's Threat Insight Team.

The threat actors behind this extensive campaign of phishing messages were previously seen in action by Proofpoint's researchers during late July at the helm of a series of phishing attacks directed at three U.S. utility companies, attempting to infect their targets with a remote access Trojan (RAT) dubbed LookBack.

During new attacks detected from August 21 through August 29, the spear-phishing emails were still trying to drop the LookBack RAT that used the same command and control (C2) center as the July sample but the hacking group's tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) have somewhat changed.

These latest phishing emails have been sent from the globalenergycertification[.]net domain, a domain designed to impersonate a utility sector licensing body.

"In this case, it masqueraded as the legitimate domain for Global Energy Certification ('GEC')," says ProofPoint's report. "The emails include a GEC examination-themed body and a malicious Microsoft Word attachment that uses macros to install and run LookBack."


Emails delivered between August 21 and August 29, 2019, purported to be an invitation to take the Global Energy Certification (“GEC”) exam administered by the Energy Research and Intelligence Institution. The email utilized the GEC logo and originated from an email address at the domain globalenergycertification[.]net which spoofs the legitimate domain globalenergycertification[.]org. - Proofpoint


Just like in the case of previous campaigns, the phishing emails come with a malicious Word document that uses VBA-based macros to infect the targets' computers with the LookBack RAT.


LoockBack phishing email

However, the threat actors have updated these VBA macros and now also attach a harmless GEC exam preparation PDF document taken from the legitimate GEC site, with the most probable purpose of increasing the messages' legitimacy and tricking the potential victims into opening the bait document.

Reconnaissance target scanning using a staging IP targeting "SMB over IP via port 445 up to two weeks prior to the arrival of phishing emails" is yet new TTP that Proofpoint chose not to disclose in their August report.

"Observed scanning IPs in some instances have also hosted phishing domains prior to their use in phishing campaigns," added the researchers.

The LookBack RAT malware

LookBack, the malware dropped in these highly-targeted attacks is a remote access Trojan developed in C++ with an impressive list of capabilities, a malicious tool designed to enable its operators to take full control of compromised machines once infected.

In their August analysis, Proofpoint found that the LookBack remote access Trojan would help the attackers to:


• enumerate services; 
• view process, system, and file data; 
• delete files; execute commands; 
• take screenshots; 
• move and click the mouse; 
• reboot the machine and delete itself from an infected host


LookBack also features multiple components, each of them used to perform different tasks, ranging from a command and control proxy tool (referred to as GUP) and a malware loader to a communications module (aka SodomNormal) used to create C&C channels with the GUP proxy tool and a remote access Trojan component (known as SodomMain).

Suspected state-sponsored hacking group behind attacks

Proofpoint said in their August report on the LookBack malware that a large number of concatenation commands were used throughout the LokBack RAT's source code to conceal what could potentially be macro strings used by advanced persistent threat (APT) groups in earlier campaigns.

This would likely allow the actors behind this spear-phishing campaign to evade anti-malware static signature detection that could be triggered by macros used other hacking groups' campaigns.



"In this instance, VBA phishing macros in Microsoft Word attachments appeared to be updated versions of macros utilized previously to target Japanese corporations in 2018," Proofpoint's senior director of threat research and detection Sherrod DeGrippo told BleepingComputer in early August.

"However, despite observing distinct similarities with historic APT campaigns, our analysts did not observe additional code overlap or infrastructure reuse that would cement attribution to a known APT group."

The group that targeted several Japanese media entities was APT10 (aka Menupass or Stone Panda) as FireEye's researchers reported in 2018, "a Chinese cyber espionage group that FireEye has tracked since 2009, and they have a history of targeting Japanese entities."

An extensive list of indicators of compromise (IOCs) including malware sample hashes, command and control server IP addresses, and domains used to deliver the targeted phishing messages is also provided at the end of Proofpoint's report.
