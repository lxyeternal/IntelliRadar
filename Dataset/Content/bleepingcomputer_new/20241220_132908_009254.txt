
	   


Police forces across the world have issued a warning for individuals turning to distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) platforms to disrupt the online activity of various organizations. Their message is simple: we are dedicated to tracking you down and bringing you to justice.

Several hundred persons have already been identified as users of such services and are now part of investigations.

The crackdown on individuals engaged in DDoS activities is a result of Operation Power Off - a collaboration between Europol and its law enforcement partners all over the globe to take down WebStresser booter/stresser website.

Police tracks down users of illegal services

WebStresser had 151,000 registered users, as the police forces discovered in April 2018 after shutting seizing the service's server infrastructure in the Netherlands, the US and Germany, and arresting the site's administrators.

The National Crime Agency (NCA) in the UK already made a number of WebSetresser users the subject of police activity. Officers have executed eight warrants since November 2018 and took hold of more than 60 computers, tablets, and mobile phones.

"A further 400 users of the service are now being targeted by the NCA and partners," NCA says in an announcement this week.

According to communication from Europol, WebStresser users are not the only ones the police have in their sight.

There is a general trend from police forces to go after users of DDoS-for-hire services all over the world. At least 20 countries, most of them in Europe, have taken this mission. The list also includes the US, Australia, and Colombia.

A crime is still a crime, no matter small

Authorities have intensified their work towards disrupting DDoS platforms, no matter their size. The user information gathered as a result of this is used to identify customers of the booter websites and the DDoS attacks they launched.

"The action taken shows that although users think that they can hide behind usernames and crypto currency, these do not provide anonymity. We have already identified further suspects linked to the site, and we will continue to take action," says Jim Stokley, Deputy Director of the NCA’s National Cyber Crime Unit.

Europol echoes the message saying that a false sense of anonymity encourages "young IT enthusiasts" to engage in what this seems to be a low-level crime.

However, DDoS attacks create victims that have to pay the price of bouncing back into the game. A report last year from Kaspersky Lab reveals estimates the financial impact a DDoS incident has on a small business could be as much as $120,000, while larger organizations could end up paying $2million on average per attack.

"Cybercrime isn’t a victimless crime and it is taken extremely seriously by law enforcement. The side effects a criminal investigation could have on the lives of these teenagers can be serious, going as far as a prison sentence in some countries," reads the press release from Europol.
