Security researchers at Microsoft say that the hacking activity started on September 16. The events preceded news from the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) about inconsistencies found in a database from Russia's national anti-doping laboratory.

Athlete doping connection

Russian athletes were part of a national systemic program for doping athletes, according to whistleblowers from the country submitting to WADA documents in support of their claims.

Fancy Bears strike again

In a brief report today, Microsoft says it discovered that the advanced threat group Strontium launched "significant cyberattacks" against various anti-doping and sporting organizations across the world.

Strontium is an elite hacker group running cyber-espionage operations believed to be at the command of the Russian government. Also known by the names APT28, Sofacy, Sednit, Tsar Team, and Sandworm.

About a year ago, a U.S. grand jury indicted seven individuals for being part of a cyber campaign to discredit international anti-doping organizations that exposed the Russian state-run doping program.

All were officers of the Russian Main Intelligence Directorate (GRU) and used the name "Fancy Bears' Hacking Team" to release stolen and false information on social media. The disinformation campaign involved contacting reporters to attract attention.

According to Microsoft, the hackers were not successful in all their recent endeavors. It is unclear how many agencies were hacked but the company says that it notified its customers affected by the attacks and worked with those seeking help to secure compromised accounts or systems.


"The methods used in the most recent attacks are similar to those routinely used by Strontium to target governments, militaries, think tanks, law firms, human rights organizations, financial firms and universities around the world."


For its intrusions, the group relies on several methods, including spear-phishing, password spray, and exploiting internet-connected devices. As for the tools, they use a mix of open-source and custom malware.

In a recent campaign attributed to this group, popular IoT devices (VOIP phone, an office printer, and a video decoder) were compromised to hop on machines of interest on corporate networks. While the purpose of these intrusions was not discovered, Fancy Bear actors are known for cyber-espionage activity.
