
	   


The AZORult information stealer and downloader malware strain was observed by Minerva Labs' research team posing as a signed Google Update installer and achieving persistence by replacing the legitimate Google Updater program on compromised machines

AZORult is an ever-evolving data-stealing Trojan also known to act as a downloader for other malware payloads in multi-stage campaigns and previously detected as part of highly complex and large scale malicious campaigns spreading ransomware, data and cryptocurrency stealing malware.

On its own, AZORult is designed to exfiltrate as much sensitive information as possible, from files, passwords, cookies, and browser history to banking credentials and cryptocurrency wallets once it successfully infects a targeted machine.

According to Minerva Labs' Asaf Aprozper and Gal Bitensky, they received a GoogleUpdate.exe binary signed with a valid certificate from a customer which was blocked by Minerva’s Anti-Evasion Platform at launch.

Everything about the GoogleUpdate.exe program pointed to it being a legitimate updater from Google, having the right icon and being signed with a non-revoked certificate.

As usual, the devil is in the details seeing that, upon closer inspection, the researchers were able to see that the binary was actually signed with a certificate issued to "Singh Agile Content Design Limited" instead of Google.


Certificate used to sign the fake GoogleUpdate binary

This certificate was issued on November 19 and, since then, it was used to sign over a hundred binaries, all of them being disguised as a GoogleUpdate executable.

Achieves persistency and administrative privileges via camouflage

After more in-depth analysis, the malware contained within the fake GoogleUpdate binary was identified as the Azorult Trojan based on multiple patterns: a HTTP POST request to a /index.php it made, using a .bit domain (for DNS over blockchain), and employing the typical Mozilla/4.0 User-Agent all Azorult infections use.

The researchers were later able to validate their conclusion using malware analysis results obtained via Intezer and VirusTotal based on a malware sample uploaded for more in-depth examination.

Besides its common data stealing habits, the malicious GoogleUpdate.exe program found on one of Minerva Labs' customers' computers comes with an extra capability: it hides in plain sight by camouflaging itself as the Google Updater program from C:\Program Files\Google\Update\GoogleUpdate.exe.

This will allow the malware to run with administrative privileges and to achieve persistence using a highly stealthy mechanism. As the researchers also stated:


Google typically defines two scheduled tasks to update its products:

GoogleUpdateTaskMachineCore – runs at login and once a day
	GoogleUpdateTaskMachineUA – runs once a day
In addition, there are two Google update services running this binary as well, as defined in the registry values:

HKEY_LOCAL_MACHINE\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Services\gupdatem\ImagePath
	HKEY_LOCAL_MACHINE\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Services\gupdate\ImagePath


Because it effectively replaces Google's updater program, this Azorult strain is able to achieve persistency without having to bother altering the Windows registry or adding any scheduled tasks of its own.

It does it by hijacking Google's already created ones, which also contributes to the infection's efficacy given that users and anti-malware solutions alike would have a harder time to notice that something suspicious is happening with the system.


Registry and scheduled tasks persistency 

As the Minerva Labs research team points out, although malware camouflaged as legitimate programs is not something new, this is the first time Azorult has been associated with this technique, another hint at the always evolving nature of this data-stealing Trojan.

As testimony of his efficiency, Azorult was recently seen distributed as a payload for the famous Fallout Exploit Kit and disseminated as part of a multi-stage sextortion campaign together with the GandCrab ransomware.

Furthermore, it was also used during a high profile and very successful phishing attack against dozens of government services from all over the world which managed to steal over 40,000 logins.
