The petition was set in motion in September 2016, just before the premiere of the Snowden movie, directed by Oliver Stone, and starring Joseph Gordon-Levitt as Snowden.

The American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU), Amnesty International, and the Human Rights Watch were behind this second effort to get Snowden pardoned.

A similar petition was signed by almost 168,000 people back in June 2013. US law states that any petition that garners over 100,000 warrants an official answer from the White House.

The White House delayed answering the first campaign for two years, eventually declining to pardon Snowden, with Homeland Security Advisor Lisa Monaco saying that Snowden should "come home to the United States, and be judged by a jury of his peers - not hide behind the cover of an authoritarian regime."

The answer is based on the custom that presidential pardons are only given to people that have been declared guilty of a crime by a court. Snowden has fled the US to Hong Kong and is now living in Russia.

Over 1.1 million signatures in less than four months

This second campaign was a tremendous success, mostly driven by the movie's success, gathering over 1.1 million signatures in less than four months.

Some of the high-profile figures that signed the petition include George Soros, the founder and chair of the Open Society Foundations; Apple co-founder Steve Wozniak; Twitter CEO Jack Dorsey; Timothy Edgar, the former director of privacy and civil liberties for the White House National Security Staff; Pentagon Papers whistleblower Daniel Ellsberg; actors Maggie Gyllenhaal, Danny Glover and Mark Ruffalo, ACLU said, in an announcement on the campaign's website.

According to several Washington insiders, President Obama is very unlikely to pardon Snowden, despite the large number of signatures. President-elect Donald Trump has spoken against Snowden and his actions in the past.

One day before the Pardon Snowden campaign announced its results, President Obama signed a new executive order that gave multiple law enforcement agencies access to NSA data, expanding the state's surveillance powers.
