
	   


SANS has shared the indicators of compromise for a recent phishing attack that compromised one of their email accounts and led to a data breach.

On August 11th, SANS disclosed that they had suffered a data breach after one of their employees fell for a phishing attack that caused 513 emails to be forwarded to attackers.

Some of the forwarded emails contained a total of approximately 28,000 records of personal information (PII) for SANS members.

SANS releases indicators of compromise

Yesterday, SANS released the indicators of compromise (IOCs) for their phishing attack so that other organizations can make sure they were not affected.

According to SANS, the initial attack started with a phishing email pretending to be a file shared by a SANS SharePoint service.

The file pretending to be shared was called "Copy of July Bonus 24JUL2020.xls," and the email prompted the user to click on the 'Open' button to access the file.


SANS phishing email

When clicked on, the button would open the default browser to "https://officei6zq49rv2p5a4xbq8ge41f1enjjczo.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws [.]com/index.html," which would immediately prompt the user to enter their Office 365 credentials.

When doing so, a Microsoft Office OAuth addon named 'Enable4Excel' would be installed.

While the site is no longer active, recipients of this phishing scam had tested this site using the Any.Run and JoeSandBox services, which gives us a small glimpse into the OAuth 'Consent' app being authorized to install.


OAuth app being installed

SANS states that once the malicious Office 365 app was installed, it would create a forwarding rule named 'Anti Spam Rule' that monitored for specific keywords.

If a matching keyword was found in an email, the email would be forwarded to an external address of 'daemon [@] daemongr5yenh53ci0w6cjbbh1gy1l61fxpd.com.'

This list of monitored financial-related keywords are:

agreement
Bank
bic
capital call
cash
Contribution
dividend
fund
iban
Payment
purchase
shares
swift
transfer
Wire
wiring info

Based on the name of the 'shared file' and numerous submissions of this hostname to VirusTotal, the phishing campaign was conducted on July 24th, 2020.

SANS was not the only target for this phishing scam, and at least two other companies had uploaded similar emails to VirusTotal.

The full indicators of compromise disclosed by SANS can be found here.

Checking for OAuth 'Consent' apps

Individual users can quickly check if they have any user consent apps or services tied to their accounts by going here.

To remove a listed consent, click on its entry, and on the page that opens, click on the 'Remove these permissions' button to remove it.



For Office 365 administrators, Microsoft has documentation on how you can check if users in your organization have installed any OAuth 'Consent' addons.

Organizations can also take several measures that should help them further protect their remote workforce from such attacks.

These steps include educating employees to spot consent phishing tactics, requiring the use of publisher verified apps, and only allow employees to OAuth apps trusted by the organization or provided by verified publishers.
