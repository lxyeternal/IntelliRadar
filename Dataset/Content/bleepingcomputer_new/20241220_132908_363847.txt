BokBot is a banking trojan also known as IcedID that emerged towards the end of 2017. Discovered by IBM's X-Force team, the malware can redirect victims to fake online banking sites or attach to a browser process to inject fake content on the original bank pages.

Security researcher Brad Duncan recently saw Trickbot with its new web inject component being downloaded by the Ursnif (a.k.a. Gozi ISFB) malware.

The host compromised in this way also receives the Trickbot variant with the BokBot/IcedID proxy module that can intercept and modify web traffic.


source: Brad Duncan

This new variant was found on a system infected on July 5 being dropped separately as "shadnewDll" and it comes with its own configuration file.


source: Brad Duncan

Duncan provides packet captures from the traffic exchanged between the infected machine and the operator's infrastructure. More information about the actions initiated by the document that spreads the malware can be gleaned from the automated analysis using the cloud-based service Any.Run.

Security researcher Vitali Kremez looked at the component and determined that it can hook to Google Chrome, Mozilla Firefox, Internet Explorer, and Microsoft Edge.

A closer inspection revealed its resemblance to BokBot's man-in-the-browser tool for injecting fake data in the traffic received by the victim user.


source: Vitali Kremez

In a thread on Twitter, Kremez notes that the interesting part is that the module appears to be adapted specifically for TrickBot or for fraudulent banking operations that rely on the installer of this malware family.

In a conversation with BleepingComputer, the researcher pointed to the striking similarity between the proxy module in Trickbot and the one used by IcedID and shown in a technical analysis from Crowdstrike published in March.


source: Vitali Kremez

The malicious add-on acts as a local proxy server positioned between the client and the online banking service. From this spot, it can insert in the connection a fake template for the bank requested by the user and collect financial information.

Seeking partners

Trickbot has always been a serious threat, ever since its appearance in 2016; but these days it is cited more frequently in reports from both the security industry and the media.

Instead of competing with other cybercriminal groups running banking malware, Trickbot's operators sought to increase their revenue stream through collaboration.

Although the relation with IcedID is not new, it is definitely tighter now. In mid-2018, researchers at Flashpoint announced that they had found computers infected with IcedID that delivered Trickbot in a next stage. The infection chain started with Emotet, which pulled in the first malware that would then download the next threat on the compromised system.

In July 2018, Brad Duncan published a more comprehensive report about Emotet and Trickbot joining forces in a malspam campaign; the relation had been known since June, though.

In January, 2019, researchers at FireEye and CrowdStrike both released reports about Trickbot being used to facilitate the spread of the Ryuk Ransomware, suggesting that a new deal may have been hammered between cybercriminal groups.

"The TrickBot administrator group, which is suspected to be based in Eastern Europe, most likely provide the malware to a limited number of cyber criminal actors to use in operations," stated FireEye's research.

The use of Emotet -> Trickbot -> Ryuk, was reported to be the same method used during the ransomware attack against the City of Lake City in Florida on June 10.
