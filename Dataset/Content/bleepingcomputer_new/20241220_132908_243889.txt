Malware authors are trolling security researchers on VirusTotal by posting abusing comments, marking their websites as dangerous sites, and voting recently discovered malicious files as "harmless."

The targets of these actions are members of MalwareHunterTeam (MHT), a group of security researchers who hunt down malware as a hobby.

MHT's activity, while helpful for the rest of us, has ruined countless of malware distribution campaigns, which MalwareHunterTeam researchers have uncovered and shared among their peers.

The place where they find, identify and catalog malicious files and malware campaigns is VirusTotal, a service that allows users to scan files and URLs for malicious content.

This is where MalwareHunterTeam and other security firms come in. They search the VirusTotal database for suspicious files, analyze the payload, and report on it.

While most companies add the newly discovered files to their virus databases, the members of the MalwareHunterTeam publish the bulk of their researcher on Twitter.

This has attracted a lot of attention, both good on bad. The good comes from the pro-bono nature of their work. The bad comes from the big bullseye they've painted on their backs.

Malware authors pestering researchers on VirusTotal

Cyber criminals haven't been shy when it comes to targeting security researchers or journalists as payback for uncovering their operations. For example, in 2015, an unknown crook has social-engineered his way into the PayPal account of infosec reporter Brian Krebs and transferred some of his funds into the PayPal account of a dead ISIS terrorist.

While none of the MalwareHunterTeam members have had their PayPal funds transferred out of their accounts, malware authors have been targeting the group in a different way.

According to the MalwareHunterTeam, for the past six months, there's been a bevy of newly-registered users on VirusTotal that have been acting strange.

These users are actively harassing the group and its members. For example, a huge number of these new users are voting as "malicious" URLs associated with the group, such as their homepage, malware trackers, or their VirusTotal profile links.





And there are many more of these user downvotes that we couldn't fit in the screenshots. These downvotes are harmless since no casual VirusTotal user would ever end up scanning those URLs, nevertheless, they take a toll on the researchers' nerves and moral.

If we investigate some of the users who are voting MalwareHunterTeam-related links as "malicious" we also see that these people are also voting malware files as "clean."

The image below (VirusTotal scan here) is a sample of the Ursnif banking trojan, which these "MalwareHunterTeam-hating users" have had no problem in deeming as a "harmless" file.



If we look closer at the activity of some of these users, we find more trollish behavior in the comments they've posted on VirusTotal.









Google, the company that owns VirusTotal, has already banned the accounts posting abusive comments, and many more, according to JamesWT, one of the MalwareHunterTeam members who's faced the brunt of their attacks.

Despite Google's actions, the security researcher expects crooks to sign up for new accounts and continue their vexatious behavior. Nevertheless, the company hasn't yet moved in to ban the accounts that have consistently voted malicious files as harmless, and vice versa, in spite of the overwhelming evidence.

It's currently unknown what triggered the crooks to start acting like this, but no doubt one of the MalwareHunterTeam members has delivered crooks a big blow in their operations last summer when this type of unique and strange cyber-bullying has started.

Until now, only members of the MalwareHunterTeam have reported this abusive behavior aimed at their VirusTotal profiles. In most cases, JamesWT was the primary target, but he's also the most active and best-ranked VirusTotal user of all-time, so he exposed more malware campaigns and annoyed more malware authors than anyone else.

This type of harassment is not only found on VirusTotal. For example, last autumn the author of the Apocalypse ransomware renamed his malware as Fabiansomware after Fabian Wosar, the Emsisoft researcher who cracked its encryption, in an attempt to pin the blame on the researcher.

These actions, which remind us of lame high-school pranks, are just the latest means through which malware authors lash out at security researchers for exposing their activities and will most likely continue as long as security researchers keep fighting the good fight.
