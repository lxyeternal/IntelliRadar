
	   


Everyone knows that major mobile service providers such as AT&T, T-Mobile, and Sprint are actively collecting their customers' location data, but not many know that they're also selling it to the highest bidder.

As discovered by Motherboard's Joseph Cox, you can locate anyone as long as you know their phone number and, of course, if you are willing to pay for it.

$300 - the price to locate a phone in the U.S.

What Cox found was that he could locate a T-Mobile phone by paying $300 to a so-called bounty hunter who was able to pinpoint the device with an accuracy of a couple of blocks.


That data access travelled through a complex chain of different companies, starting with T-Mobile, before going to a location aggregator called Zumigo. Zumigo then sold it to a company called Microbilt, which provides the access to a variety of industries, including bounty hunters. A bounty hunter then sold it to a source, and that source finally provided the phone’s location to Motherboard.


Following Cox's report, AT&T decided to completely shut down its location data sharing services, while T-Mobile' CEO John Legere stated in a post on Twitter that his company will also gradually end these practices until March.



AT&T also said in a CNET statement that "Last year we stopped most location aggregation services while maintaining some that protect our customers, such as roadside assistance and fraud prevention. In light of recent reports about the misuse of location services, we have decided to eliminate all location aggregation services—even those with clear consumer benefits."

US senators and the FCC Commissioner ask for further investigations

Motherboard's report is not the first one uncovering the shady practices of US wireless companies, with U.S. Senator Ron Wyden coming to the same conclusion after a June 2018 investigation which led to both AT&T and Verizon promising they will end their contracts with location aggregators.

Wyden said at the time that:


Chairman Pai’s total abandonment of his responsibility to protect Americans’ security shows that he can’t be trusted to oversee an investigation into the shady companies that he used to represent. If your location information falls into the wrong hands, you – or your children – can be vulnerable to predators, thieves and a whole host of people who would use that knowledge to malicious ends.


Despite the Senator's harsh words, FCC Commissioner Jessica Rosenworcel confirmed the Federal Communications Commission's possible involvement as part of a future inquiry into Motherboard's new findings via a short tweet saying "The @fcc needs to investigate. Stat."

Rosenworcel also stated during an MSBNC program that "It shouldn't be that you pay a few hundred dollars to a bounty hunter and then they can tell you in real time where a phone is within a few hundred meters. That’s not right. This entire ecosystem needs some oversight. I think we’ve got to get to this fast."

Carriers have been caught selling location data before

Selling their customers' location data has always been a lucrative practice for carriers, seeing that every year since at least 2013 (2014, 2015, 2016, 2017) they've been caught spreading it around to any company willing to pay.

Maybe something will change in 2019 and they will keep their promise to stop doing it, which they might have to if Senator Wyden's Consumer Data Protection Act of 2018 Discussion Draft proposed in November 2018 will be passed by the US Congress in the form of a bill.
