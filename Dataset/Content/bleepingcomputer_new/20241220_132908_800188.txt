
	   


Believe it or not, there are DVRs and IP cameras available online today that you could hack just like in the movies, in a matter of seconds.

These devices belong to Dahua, a Chinese manufacturer, who released firmware updates for different products after a security researcher found a laughably simple way of hacking their devices.

Vulnerability is easy to exploit

The security researcher, going by the name of Bashis, says Dahua DVRs and IP cameras store their configurations on a web server. This configuration file is stored in the same location and can be downloaded by anyone that knows the camera or DVR's IP address.

The attacker only has to access this URL in his browser to download it onto his PC. The configuration file stores information on all user accounts with access to the device. A hacker only needs to select the user he likes and access the device.

Attacks can be automated

Attackers can automate all these operations using curl or wget commands to download the file, then use parsers to read its content, and shell scripts to automate the login operations. After getting access to the device, they can do whatever they want, from uploading malware to reflashing the entire device with new firmware.

Earlier this week, Bashis disclosed his findings and even published proof-of-concept code on GitHub. Dahua later asked the researcher to remove the exploit code for at least a month, to give customers time to update their devices.

A day later, the vendor also published new firmware for eleven product models.

Dahua devices have been a breeding ground for IoT botnets

During the past two years, Dahua devices have often been mentioned as one of the top targets of IoT malware. Dahua DVRs have been one of the main culprits behind the recent DDoS attacks on KrebsOnSecurity and Dyn, a managed DNS provider [1, 2]. Both attacks were some of the largest known to date.

The botnets that leveraged Dahua devices used an older Dahua flaw, CVE-2013-6117, which was a three-year-old vulnerability at the time of the attacks.

This means that despite the new Dahua firmware releases, it will take years until device owners update their DVRs and cameras, leaving them exposed online. Knowing how simple this exploit is, expect to see more Dahua devices as part of IoT botnets in the coming future.
