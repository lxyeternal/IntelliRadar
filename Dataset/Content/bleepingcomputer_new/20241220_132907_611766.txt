The developer of the 42 apps that made it into the official Android store added some tricks that made it more difficult to identify the source of the adware on devices.

Using publicly available information, security researchers were able to determine his identity, including his education level and college grades, social media profiles, and activity as a mobile app developer.

Lukas Stefanko, mobile malware researcher at ESET, discovered 42 apps in Google Play that were part of a large adware campaign running since at least July 2018.

To achieve their ad-spewing purpose, the apps kept this behavior hidden until it passed all verification from Google. This was possible by checking if the IP address of the device running them belonged to Google. If true, the adware payload would remain silent.

Further effort to keep the adware functions under the radar translated into a 24-minute delay for showing the first ad. A typical testing procedure for an Android app takes 10 minutes tops, so the software would likely pass this verification.



This would also keep the user in the dark about the app that exhibited the unwanted behavior as it would more difficult to associate it with a particular app, Stefanko writes in a blog post today.

For resilience, the author resorted to the old trick of showing a shortcut icon for the app. A user trying to uninstall the app from the main screen would only delete the shortcut and the software would keep running in the background.

Another tactic observed in these apps for concealing the origin of the ads was to mimic legitimate apps from Facebook or Google.

"If the user wants to check which app is responsible for the ad being displayed, by hitting the “Recent apps” button, another trick is used: the app displays a Facebook or Google icon" - Lukas Stefanko

Few users know that long-pressing an icon in the "Recent Apps" list shows the real name of the app.



Of the 42 apps discovered by ESET, one in particular generated over five million installations. It was a video downloader that garnered about 87,000 reviews averaging a 4.2 rating.

It must be noted that the apps included the functionality advertised in their description and adware acted as a secondary effect. This helped them stay undetected for such a long time.



Dev fails to hide his traces

It appears that the developer of these apps was as good at hiding the adware behavior as he was bad at hiding personal details leading to his identification.

The researchers started from the command and control (C2) server. Registration details contained not only a name but also a country and an email address.



Most of the times this information is fake but in this case it pointed to an individual that was on the list of students at a university in Vietnam. This record included a phone number that fit the one provided for domain registration.

The email address matched the details in a GitHub profile of an Android developer. Although the repository did not have any code related to the adware found on Google Play, at one point it did have a project called "TestDelete" that included the adware package.

More sleuthing uncovered a YouTube channel that promoted the adware and other projects. A Facebook profile recorded in one of the videos advertising the malicious package confirmed that the owner studied at the university found in an earlier stage of the investigation.

"Linked on the malicious developer’s Facebook profile, we discovered a Facebook page, Minigameshouse, and an associated domain, minigameshouse[.]net. This domain is similar to the one the malware author used for his adware C&C communication, minigameshouse[.]us," says Stefanko.

Registration for the two domains have the same phone number, which is also listed on the Facebook profile.



Some of the adware apps in Google Play were also in Apple's App Store but did not include the annoying behavior.

According to ESET telemetry, the adware apps from this particular developer were distributed across the world to users in at least 24 countries.

Adware is not harmful in the way malware is but it does have a negative impact on user experience and the device itself. They are annoying and increase the battery discharge rate as well as mobile traffic; they also collect personal information and device fingerprinting details.

ESET reported the apps to Google and they are no longer present in Play Store.
