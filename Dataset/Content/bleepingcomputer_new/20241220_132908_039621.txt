
	   


A month after it suffered a mysterious cyber attack, details emerged that San Francisco’s public TV and radio station, KQED, was the victim of a ransomware incident from which it has yet to fully recover.

Based on early reporting covering the event [1, 2], the incident took place on June 15, when an unknown strain of ransomware infected one of KQED's Internet-connected computers, spreading to other workstations on the same network.

Ransomware infection rakes radio stream offline for 12 hours

The ransomware locked data on crucial systems, and the radio's station live stream went off the air for 12 hours from Thursday evening to 9:30 AM the next morning, according to a network status update.

KQED still recovering from ransomware infection

According to reporters from the San Francisco Chronicle, one month later, KQED is still reeling from the attack.

KQED CTO Dan Mansergh told the Chronicle they decided to wipe all affected computers and reinstall their operating systems. Things have been moving slowly, and the IT staff hasn't been able to fix all affected computers.

For example, the wireless network at KQED's headquarters was down for days after the ransomware hit, while IT staff took nearly two weeks to restore the station's email server.

For two weeks, KQED TV broadcasts were moved and recorded at a studio at the University of California, Hastings. Even now, the team uses a stopwatch to time TV segments, a task previously automated by special software.

Furthermore, new hires weren't able to start work because KQED couldn't issue new access cards.

KQED is not the only company that's having problems recovering from ransomware attacks. FedEx is in a similar situation. For three weeks the courier service has been trying to recover from the NotPetya ransomware outbreak. The company hasn't restored full service to some of the affected branches and has admitted it may not be able to recover all of the lost data.

Other ransomware infections reported on June 15

KQED hasn't yet figured out how it got infected in the first place, but there are enough clues online to reveal what might have happened.

The day on which the radio station was infected, June 15, was a significant day in terms of ransomware attacks.

On that day, two universities from the UK were infected with ransomware via a massive malvertising campaign known as AdGholas. Both Ulster University and University College London had to shut down a large part of their network due to a ransomware infection.

In addition, the Waverly Health Center in Iowa (the US) was also infected with ransomware on the same day, June 15.

In a report, Proofpoint confirmed it saw the AdGholas target both the UK and the US with malicious ads. These ads redirected users to the Astrum exploit kit, which then used browser exploits to install the Mole ransomware on victims' computers.

Ironically, weeks before it got hit with ransowmare, KQED was reporting on ways to avoid ransomware infections.
