In a short period, the crew launched at least 26 attacks targeting 14 organizations, all the while keeping a very low profile.

Staying under the radar was possible by using custom tools and resorting to tactics similar to red team activity tests an organization’s defenses against cyberattacks.

Named RedCurl, the earliest known activity from this threat actor is group is from May 2018. The group is believed to be Russian speaking and was mentioned for the first time last year, in a report from Group-IB, a Singapore-based cybersecurity company.

Since then, the researchers documented the tactics, tools, and infrastructure used by RedCurl from multiple incident response engagements and published them in a report today.

Among the victims of this group companies in the construction, consulting, financial, retail, tourism, legal, and insurance sectors. Their geography spans from Russia, Ukraine, United Kingdom, Norway, and Germany to Canada.



In one incident response, Group-IB found data linked to an employee at a cybersecurity company. Although the IP addresses communicating with RedCurl infrastructure belonged to the company, it is difficult to determine if this was the result of a compromise or researchers researchers running their analysis of the attacker’s malware.

Since their operations were discovered, RedCurl was most active in 2019, when then they deployed well over a dozen attacks between June and December in all the aforementioned countries.

Attacks start with well-written phishing emails often impersonating human resource staff at the targeted organization. Sometimes the same message (e.g. about annual bonuses) was delivered to multiple individuals working in the department of the attacker’s interest.

Group-IB says that the group has strong social engineering skills, using the victim company’s domain name in the sender address and making the messages look legitimate with the official company logo.

Cloud’s the way

RedCurl delivers their payloads via links to archives stored at multiple public cloud storage services. In 2020, the actor switched to LNK (shortcuts) and XLAM (Excel Macro-Enabled add-on) files.



When victims launched these files, “an attacker-controlled cloud storage was set up on the local system as a network drive and launched RedCurl.Dropper, which was hosted there, after which a phishing document was displayed to the victim,” Group-IB discovered.

In the attacks they investigated, the researchers found that the actor used Dropbox and a vast number of other free storage services: cloudme.com, koofr.net, pcloud.com, idata.uz, drivehq.com, driveonweb.de, opendrive.com, powerfolder.com, docs.live.net, syncwerk.cloud, cloud.woelkli.com, and framagenda.org.

RedCurl uses the MultCloud platform for managing and accessing the storage space from these services, which also serve command and control purposes via PowerShell scripts.



Group-IB researchers’ assessment is that RedCurl is out to steal documentation and business emails from the its victims. This is achieved by sending a list of folders and documents available from the compromised machine to the malware operator, who decides which data should be exfiltrated.

At the same time, all JPG, PDF, DOC, DOCX, XLS, and XLSX files on network drives are replaced with malicious LNKs that deploy RedCurl.Dropper. This propagates the attack to other machines inside the target organization.

The threat actor also uses the LaZagne open-source tool (obfuscated with PyArmor) to steal credentials, for email in particular, stored in memory and in web browsers. If this fails, a PowerShell script deploys a phishing window impersonating Microsoft Outlook.


“After gaining access to the victim's email, RedCurl uses another PowerShell script to analyze and upload all documents of interest to cloud storages” - Group-IB


RedCurl spends plenty of time inside a victim network, between two to six months, allowing them to sift through data of interest and move laterally with the lightest steps.

The use of legitimate services and tools, PowerShell, and custom malware helps RedCurl remain undetected.

Group-IB believes that this adversary’s skills are commissioned for corporate espionage, an activity that does not have a direct financial impact but which can result in losses of millions of dollars.

RedCurl’s incursions have benefited from the lack of indicators and technical data, allowing the actor to carry the attack to more evolved stages, says Rustam Mirkasymov.

A technical report is now available from Group-IB, with indicators of compromise that could help organizations detect intrusions from this actor at an early phase.
