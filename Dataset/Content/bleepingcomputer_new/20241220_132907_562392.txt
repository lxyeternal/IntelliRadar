Operations of nation-state hackers and cybercriminal groups are typically distinct from one another but the new findings suggest that these threat actors may cooperate towards a financially-motivated goal.

TrickBot's extensive capabilities

Since its discovery in 2016, TrickBot added a score of capabilities well beyond the initial banking trojan purposes. Malware delivery, network profiling, and extended data collection are some of the modules that make it a flexible crimeware solution fitting all sorts of needs.

A TrickBot variant referred to as Anchor, first seen on VirusTotal in July 2018, stands out as targeting high-value victims for financial reasons.

It communicates with the command and control (C2) server over DNS and according to an analysis from NTT security company it targets organizations in the financial sector.

SentinelLABS, the freshly launched research division from SentinelOne, define Anchor as a framework of pieces that "allow the actors to leverage [it] against their higher-profile victims."

Anchor uses both custom and already available tools and one of its components is an uninstaller that removes traces of the infection, leaving forensic analysis teams with little to investigate.

According to SentinelLABS' findings, the Anchor server hosts multiple tools that serve the interests of both cybercriminal and nation-state actors.



When goals align

For cybercriminals, TrickBot's Anchor server can deliver stealers, fraud kits, and ransomware, while APT groups can take advantage of reconnaissance info and persistence. Some state actors, though, are also interested in making money.

The researchers were able to recover artifacts from a machine infected with the Anchor variant and found a task that reached to ecombox[.]store.



Through the task above, the malware module on the infected machine downloaded the PowerRatankba, a PowerShell-based malware linked to Lazarus that enables deploying a multifunctional backdoor.



The same domain was seen in an attack against the Chilean Redbanc in December 2018. Analysis from Flashpoint shows that the PowerRatankba malware, attributed to Lazarus group, called a server at that location to download a next-stage attack tool.

This finding reveals what looks like a business relationship between TrickBot and the Lazarus nation-state actor, which is interested in financial gain and collecting information for future operations. TrickBot accommodates both needs.

Lazarus is well-known for the financial motivation behind its operations, which include attacks against financial institutions in  India, Mexico, Pakistan, Philippines, South Korea, Taiwan, Turkey, Chile, and Vietnam.

The most famous heist attributed to this threat actor targeted the Bangladesh Central Bank, which caused losses of $81 million out of an attempted transfer of $1 billion.

Vitali Kremez, the head of SentinelLABS, writes that TrickBot opening its doors to APT actors sets a new milestone in the evolution of cybercrime.


"In this ecosystem, crimeware and APT are no longer siloed; on the opposite, each type of crime creates added value for the other, each becomes a force multiplier" - Vitali Kremez


It is unclear if this collaboration is intentional or not but one thing is clear: TrickBot managed to evolve once more, creating an enterprise open to any threat actor willing to pay the price for using its tools, infrastructure, and big data carefully processed to allow selection of high-profile targets.
