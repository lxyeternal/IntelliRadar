This malware was first spotted and analyzed by G-Data in 2014, while another trojan featuring "strong code similarities" capable of carrying out man-in-the-middle (MitM) attacks on encrypted traffic was discovered by Kaspersky in 2019, which later dubbed it Reductor.

Even though G-Data did not attribute COMpfun to any specific malware author, Kaspersky associates it "with the Turla APT with a medium-to-low level of confidence" based on the victims its operators are targeting.

The new COMpfun malware variant was discovered by Kaspersky in November 2019 and it comes with all the capabilities all RAT malware features.

Once it infects a target's system, it starts collecting information for its masters, packs it all up, and sends it to its command and control (C2) servers.

COMpfun harvests geolocation info and system data, it logs window titles and all keystrokes on compromised systems, and it takes screenshots that allow it to capture sensitive information from the victim's screen.


COMpfun infection chain (Kaspersky)

However, unlike other RATs, this one also has the ability to propagate to other (potentially air-gapped) devices by monitoring for and infecting any removable devices connected to the compromised devices.

"If initialization is successful, the malware starts one more thread for dispatching Windows messages, looking for removable devices related to a WM_DEVICECHANGE event," Kaspersky explains.

"The module runs its own handlers in the event of a USB device being plugged into or unplugged from the host.""


Trojan capabilities (Kaspersky)

Malware controlled using HTTP status codes

The most interesting addition to this new COMpfun version is the HTTP status-based communication module which allows the malware operators to bypass detection by avoiding to use known malicious traffic patterns.

"We observed an interesting C2 communication protocol utilizing rare HTTP/HTTPS status codes (check IETF RFC 7231, 6585, 4918)," Kaspersky says.

Depending on the HTTP status code received by systems infected with COMpfun malware, the attackers can request them to exfiltrate all the collected data to the C2 server, to gain persistence, fingerprint the hosts, to enumerate network resources, propagate to USB removable devices, and more.

"The malware operators retained their focus on diplomatic entities and the choice of a visa-related application – stored on a directory shared within the local network – as the initial infection vector worked in their favor," Kaspersky concludes.

"The combination of a tailored approach to their targets and the ability to generate and execute their ideas certainly makes the developers behind COMPFun a strong offensive team."

ESET researchers have uncovered another new malware toolkit they named Ramsay, also capable of infecting air-gapped systems by abusing removable drives and network shares, and of exfiltrating files it harvests via Internet-connected computers on the same network.

More details and information on the inner workings of the COMpfun RAT, as well as indicators of compromise (IOCs), are available within Kaspersky's full report on the newly discovered variant.
