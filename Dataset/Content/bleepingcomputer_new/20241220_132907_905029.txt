The tally for health records exposed or lost to a cyber incident this year is now pushing closer to 40 million.

Data breach spike

Throughout October, the U.S. Department of Health & Human Services' (HHS) Office for Civil Rights was notified of 52 data breaches that impacted hundreds of thousands of health records.

The count of exposed, lost, or stolen health records until the end of October this year passed the 38 million mark, HIPAA Journal reports.

While the figure is impressive compared to the past three years (more than three times the count registered in 2018 and more than seven times the records impacted in the previous year), it is far from being the largest.

The undefeated record was logged in 2015, the year when the Anthem and Premera Blue Cross health insurers declared data breaches (1, 2) that compromised personal information belonging to 78.8 million and 11 million customers respectively.



Last month, providers of healthcare services and plans, and businesses related to the sector in 24 states reported data breaches to HHS' Office for Civil Rights. Most of the incidents, 17 of them, were in Texas.

The top two organizations with the highest largest number of records affected were Betty Jean Kerr People’s Health Centers (152,000) and  Kalispell Regional Healthcare (140,209).

However, in the case of Betty Jean the records do not appear to have been exposed to an unauthorized party but lost to a ransomware attack, so the files were encrypted.

The organization decided not to pay the ransom to recover them and could not restore them from backups.

28 of the incidents were caused by unauthorized access or disclosure, while 18 of them originated from hacking or IT incidents. This shows that the healthcare industry is still a target that is both appealing and easy to attack.



HHS' Office for Civil Rights (OCR) also issued financial penalties for some of the entities affected by the cyber incidents. One of them ended with a settlement for publicly disclosing health information in response to a review on Yelp.

Following an investigation, OCR also established that the practice did not include sufficient information in its notice of privacy practices. Both parties agreed to $10,000 settlement.

Another ended with a civil monetary sanction of more than $2 million for allowing public disclosure of a photo of an operating room display that showed health details of two individuals, one of them being "a well-known NFL star."
