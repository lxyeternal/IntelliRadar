
	   


The author of the Rakos malware has built himself a nicely-sized botnet, according to a post today by Renato Marinho of Morphus Labs.

Based on data gathered via a series of "high interaction" honeypots, Marinho says the Rakos botnet lists around 8,300 active bots every day, and over 24,000 unique bots over a three-day period.

While the numbers are significantly lower than what we've come accustomed from botnets lately, 8,300 daily bots are more than enough to launch large DDoS attacks without too much effort.

The Rakos botnet, on the other hand, has not yet been seen carrying out any DDoS attacks. For now, the bot herder is busy expanding his botnet to more and more devices.

Rakos botnet evolves, grows stronger

Rakos is just one of the latest IoT malware families spotted by researchers. The malware first started spreading in August 2016, but the first report on its modus operandi was published in December by ESET.

According to Marinho, the Rakos malware has evolved in the last few months. For example, the malware now uses a P2P structure, where some bots act as command-and-control (C&C) servers, while others as "checkers."

This last category of bots focuses only on launching brute-force attacks on open SSH ports, which helps the botnet grow to new devices.

On the other hand, bots that act as C&C servers are called "skaros," and they're crucial to the entire structure, as it allows the botnet herder to change it's main C&C server at will, without losing control over his entire network.

According to Marinho, around 10% of the bots act as C&C servers, while the rest are "checkers."

You can remove Rakos malware by rebooting your device

Right now, Rakos is just another IoT botnet slowly building mass in the shadows, ready for its author to weaponize its bots with DDoS or other malicious features.

Currently, most of the Rakos bots are Raspberry Pi devices (45%), but the botnet's composition changes on a daily basis because bots aren't persistent and just like most IoT malware, the infection is removed when rebooting the device.

Despite this, there have been many studies that showed that an IoT device, left unprotected, will be compromised within minutes. The best advice is to secure any device left exposed online with a secure, hard-to-guess password.
