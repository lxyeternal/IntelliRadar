Between August 30 and October 5 last year, operators of the 'Aaron Smith' sextortion email campaigns made almost 150,000 in bitcoin (~23 BTC) from victims. Since the beginning of 2019, profits decreased to about $17,000 (~3.5 BTC).

Bag of tricks

To relive the glory days, the scammers resorted to some simple tricks in an attempt to get their messages past the spam filters. Looking at the email below as interpreted by the email client, one would not suspect that it does not use normal lettering.

A deeper analysis of the message reveals a more complex underlying code that mixes plain text letters with HTML character entities. The recipient sees only the rendering of the email client.



Researchers at Cisco Talos say that such a trick is not guaranteed to evade spam detection completely, though.

As such, the scammers turned the page in their book of tricks to a method that's been known since 2005: embedding images of the message in the email body.

By doing so, the anti-spam protection mechanism has little information to assess. However, the scammers failed to anticipate one thing: the bitcoin address where victims should pay the money (as much as $7,000) is a random string of characters and copying it by hand may result in errors, potentially lowering the chance of the extortionists to cash in.

'Proof' of misbehavior

Sextortion emails are nothing but a blackmail scam that preys on the naivety of the victim, who is easily duped by the presence of a personal password (taken from public data dumps) in the message.

In more recent campaigns, the scammers play the social engineering card once again. To convince the victim that the blackmail is real and there is evidence of their manual override, the messages purport to deliver video proof of the act in a ZIP file.

"When extracted, this file prompts the user to visit a URL, and then extracts a file, "Password.txt," that contains the same URL and instructions, plus another ZIP file" that purports to be the video, explained Nick Biasini and Jaeson Schultz.

The victim will never get to the alleged video, though, because it is a password-protected archive. This is just a game to make them follow the links, where they are asked to pay $50 to get the unlock password, a sum that victims are more likely to pay in order to see what compromising evidence there is on them.



"Another campaign switched from attaching ZIP files to PDFs. These PDFs then link to a webpage, which is redirecting to a Cryptonator — a cryptocurrency wallet — portal requesting $25 for the ZIP file. This may be an indication that the initial effort of sending a ZIP file directly is not paying off as well as expected," note the researchers.



This type of attacks can be avoided if you rely on strong, unique passwords that are difficult to crack. Adding two-factor authentication (2FA) protection layer to online accounts, where available, is also a good step to make sure that cybercriminals do not get access to your private data.
