
	   


The TA505 hacking group ran a spear phishing campaign targeting a financial institution during April with the help of a signed version of the ServHelper backdoor and a number of LOLBins designed to help the operation evade detection.

TA505 is a threat group known to have been active since at least Q3 2014 [1, 2] and to have attacked a multiple financial institutions and retail companies using large sized malicious spam campaigns driven with the help of the Necurs botnet and dropping the Dridex and Trick banking Trojans, as well as the Locky and Jaff ransomware strains on their targets computers. [1, 2, 3]

During November 2018, TA505 started distributing new malicious tools as discovered by Proofpoint, the ServHelper backdoor and the FlawedGrace remote access Trojan (RAT) as part of multiple malware campaigns focused on banks, retail businesses, and restaurants.

Cybereason's security researchers found that TA505's highly targeted phishing attacks made use of  "selective persistence mechanism and self kill commands based on autonomous reconnaissance," as well as "deliberate timeline, indicated by the timing of the phishing attack and signing of the malicious code" using a legitimate certificate from Sectigo RSA Code Signing CA.

To be more exact, "the malware was signed mere hours prior to the attack" and, as detailed by Cybereason, gaining persistence will only be attempted after receiving a command from the attackers' command-and-control servers, achieving persistence by creating an "Intel Protect" registry key under "HKCU\Software\Mcirosoft\Windows\Current Version\Run:"


Whereas malware will usually attempt to gain persistence whenever possible, the tools being used in this operation create persistence based on their environment. The tools will collect information about the target machine and send this information to a remote C2 server, which will decide whether to set up persistence. This is a technique commonly used in advanced operations.


Evasion and evidence removal

While Cybereason was able to successfully contain the attack against the financial institution, the researchers still managed to do a breakdown of the spear phishing campaign designed to disseminate the ServHelper backdoor as its end goal, aiming for specific "machines and users in order to maximize potential damage."

The APT group's attack made use of LOLBins (examples available HERE), taking advantage of legitimate and native Windows binaries (msiexec.exe, rundll32.exe, and net.exe) to deliver its ServHelper malware payload in an attempt to achieve an extra level of stealthiness, an effective tactic as shown by a previous analysis of an attack targeting Brazilian entities.


msiexec.exe used to download the first stage payload


LOLBins are deceptive because their execution seems benign at first, or even sometimes safe. In addition, the use of a signed and verified file with certification increases the likelihood that the malware will stay under the radar.


ServHelper also allows the attackers to collect the info needed to decide if the removal of all evidence left on the compromised machines is required, demonstrating "the level of care taken in this operation, and indicates at the threat actor’s potential goal- to plant a backdoor that would remain undetected for as long as possible in order to more effectively exfiltrate data."

Malware modules

The malware comes with multiple modules designed to perform highly specific tasks. For instance, nsExec.dll is used by the attackers to launch binaries from the command-line and capture their output without the need to actually open a DOSBox.

This module "gives the attacker the ability to execute the command line and run the rundll32.exe process without appearing on the target machines desktop" and it also "increases the stealthiness of the malware when executing commands from the Windows command line" according to Cybereason's extensive analysis.

The malware's main module, pegas.dll, makes it possible to take advantage of the ServHelper backdoor's full capabilities, including "reconnaissance, information stealing, and backdoor capabilities. In addition, this module is also responsible for communicating with another C2 server that determines the next steps for the malware."

Using ServHelper, the attackers target the highest priority machines in the organization by only collecting info from computers which have the "S-1-5-32-544" SID identifier set for "the local administrator and all local and domain administrators user groups."


Searching for machines with Admin SIDs

Additionally, like most other backdoors, ServHelper will go into a sleep state until its masters want to "to activate additional malicious activities."

Cybereason's analysis also details that the backdoor is capable of downloading and executing additional malware modules with the help of the rundll32.exe binary, as well as self-terminating and cleaning all traces of the malware infection from the compromised machines.

"As the use of LOLBins becomes more commonplace, we suspect this complex method of attack will become more widely used as well. The potential for damage will grow as attackers look to other, more destructive payloads," Cybereason concludes.

Update April 25 13:05 EDT: Tim Callan, Senior Fellow at Sectigo sent in an official statement:


As a policy Sectigo revokes certificates used in malware attacks and does not issue certificates to known malware purveyors.  We encourage security researchers to report instances of malware employing Sectigo certificates at signedmalwarealert@sectigo.com.
