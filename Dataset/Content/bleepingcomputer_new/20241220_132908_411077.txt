Although the attack infrastructure used in previous QSnatch attacks (from early 2014 to mid-2017 and from late 2018 to late 2019) is currently not active, the two agencies urge all QNAP customers to update their NAS devices as soon as possible to block future campaigns.

62,000 QNAP devices infected worldwide

"All QNAP NAS devices are potentially vulnerable to QSnatch malware if not updated with the latest security fixes," the two agencies explain (1, 2).

The two agencies have found roughly 62,000 infected devices worldwide in mid-June 2020, of which about 7,600 were found in the United States and 3,900 in the United Kingdom.

To block QSnatch malware infections, organizations are advised to follow the recommendations listed in QNAP's security advisory on Qsnatch malware, including updating the QTS NAS OS, Malware Remover, and Security Counselor to the latest available versions.

CISA and NCSC also share the following mitigations to prevent future attacks:

• Verify that you purchased QNAP devices from reputable sources. If sources are in question, run a full factory reset on the device prior to completing the firmware upgrade.
• Block external connections when the device is intended to be used strictly for internal storage.

QSnatch malware capabilities

QNAP warned its customers of ongoing attacks trying to infect their NAS devices with QSnatch malware in November 2019 after security researchers at the National Cyber Security Centre of Finland (NCSC-FI) discovered thousands of infected QNAP NAS devices.

As they found, QSnatch can steal user credentials found on compromised NAS devices and inject malicious code retrieved from its command and control (C2) servers.

QSnatch comes with multiple capabilities including but not limited to:


• a CGI password logger used to install a fake device admin login page for phishing credentials
• a credential scrapper
• an SSH backdoor to enable the attackers to run arbitrary code on compromised devices
• a web shell to provide remote access
• exfiltration module for stealing a pre-defined list of files (including logs and system configuration)


Once it infects a QNAP NAS device, QSnatch will also block software updates to gain persistence by editing cronjob and init scripts, preventing Malware Remover from running, and overwriting update sources and redirecting update domains and URLs to local versions.

While the infection vector is still unknown (with the exception of the fact that QSnatch is injected into the device firmware), the agencies mention that some of the samples found on infected NAS devices will "intentionally patch the infected QNAP for Samba remote code execution vulnerability CVE-2017-7494."

This is either part of an attempt to block others from taking control of the devices after they ger infected with QSnatch or is a sign of a potential infection vector used in previous attacks to deploy the malware.

QNAP NAS devices under fire

QSnatch is not the first instance when QNAP's NAS devices have been under attack with the company issuing a security advisory during early October 2019 about devices running phpMyAdmin with weak SQL server passwords being struck by Muhstik Ransomware.

In August 2019, QNAP got another advisory out about an eCh0raix Ransomware (also known as QNAPCrypt) series of attacks targeting QNAP devices with weak passwords and outdated QTS firmware.

One week earlier, ransomware expert BloodDolly released a decryptor for some variants of the eCh0raix ransomware strain in a BleepingComputer support topic.

During May 2018, QNAP also informed its customers of ongoing VPNFilter malware attacks attempting to infect NAS devices with default administrator account passwords or running QTS 4.2.6 build 20170628, 4.3.3 build 20170703, and earlier versions.
