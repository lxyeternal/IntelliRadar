
	   


Twitter announced today the introduction of a new "Hide replies" feature to its platform in June, providing users with more control over the conversations stemming from their tweet.

The "Hide replies" option was initially unearthed by mobile researcher Jane Manchun Wong back in February, a report which was confirmed by Twitter Senior Product Manager Michelle Yasmeen Haq on the same day.

As Haq said at the moment, "With this feature, the person who started a conversation could choose to hide replies to their tweets. The hidden replies would be viewable by others through a menu option. [..] We think the transparency of the hidden replies would allow the community to notice and call out situations where people use the feature to hide content they disagree with."

The company also said that "38% of abusive content that’s enforced is surfaced proactively to our teams for review instead of relying on reports from people on Twitter," allowing the social network to react to toxic behavior on the platform without having to rely only on user reports.


People who don’t feel safe on Twitter shouldn’t be burdened to report abuse to us. Previously, we only reviewed potentially abusive Tweets if they were reported to us. We know that’s not acceptable, so earlier this year we made it a priority to take a proactive approach to abuse in addition to relying on people’s reports.


More to the point, "potentially abusive content" such as "abusive behavior, hateful conduct, encouraging self-harm, and threats, including those that may be violent" is now automatically flagged allowing Twitter's human review team remove anything that breaks the social network's Terms of Service.

The tech used to keep track of platform manipulation, spam and various other rules violations is also behind the automated flagging of abusive content.


Today, by using technology, 38% of abusive content that’s enforced is surfaced proactively for human review instead of relying on reports from people using Twitter.
— Twitter Safety (@TwitterSafety) April 16, 2019

45% more users suspended for creating new accounts after suspension

Also, since last year's report on Twitter's public conversation health, "100,000 accounts suspended for creating new accounts after a suspension during January-March 2019 –– a 45% increase from the same time last year."

Twitter also improved its reactions times to appeal requests received from users with roughly 60% faster response with the help of the platform's new in-app appeal process, while also being able to suspend 300% more abusive accounts within 24 hours when compared to the previous report.

The social network also said in this year's platform health update that it counted "16% fewer abuse reports after an interaction from an account the reporter doesn’t follow."

In addition, Twitter's team was also able to have "2.5 times more private information removed with a new, easier reporting process" which allows the social network's users to provide more information regarding the tweets reported to share personal information.


From January-March, 100,000 accounts were suspended for creating new accounts after a suspension –– a 45% increase from the same time last year.
— Twitter Safety (@TwitterSafety) April 16, 2019

Twitter also listed a number of changes that will be coming to the platform during the next few months:


We’ll continue to improve our technology to help us review content that breaks our rules faster and before it’s reported, specifically those who Tweet private information, threats, and other types of abuse.
	We’ll make it easier for people who use Twitter to share specifics when reporting so we can take action faster, especially when it comes to protecting people’s physical safety.
	Context on Tweets and our enforcement is important in understanding our rules, so we’ll add more notices within Twitter for clarity, such as if a Tweet breaks our rules but remains on the service because the content is in the public interest.
	We are updating our rules in the next few weeks so they’re shorter, simpler and easier to understand.
