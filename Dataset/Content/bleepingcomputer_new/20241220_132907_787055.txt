
	   


A new exploit kit that researchers named Spelevo has emerged recently targeting a certain category of victims and infect their computer systems with two banking trojans.

To achieve their goal, exploit kits (EKs) use a traffic direct system (TDS) or gate that points the connection to a landing page where the potential victim device is analyzed for vulnerable applications. Candidates are then directed to the adequate exploit.

Spelevo exploits patched bugs in IE and Adobe Flash

The latest exploits preferred by these browser-based threats are Internet Explorer’s CVE-2018-8174, and Flash’s CVE-2018-15982 and CVE-2018-4878, as noted by Malwarebytes most recent in their most recent seasonal EK report.

If a vulnerable version of Adobe Flash plugin is available, Spelevo leverages the first bug (CVE-2018-15982) Malwarebytes mentioned for the component; otherwise, the IE flaw (CVE-2018-8174) is exploited, which is a use-after-free in the VBScript engine of the browser.



Cisco Talos researchers assess that the Spelevo campaign they investigated originated at a B2B contact website; this may indicate that its targets are among the business' customers.

Talos Outreach director Craig Williams told BleepingComputer that anyone visiting the compromised site could have become a victim if they also had vulnerable software on the system.

According to some statistics, Internet Explorer still accounts for up to 5% of the browser market share.

"This website initially appeared to just have a single page compromised, but as the investigation continued, it appeared that the compromise was more significant with multiple pages, including the main homepage redirecting to the gate used for the campaign."

While the researchers' were conducting their investigation, the operator of the site was already aware of the compromise, which required only a few lines of code, and was working to fix things.



The infection process starts with loading a domain (ezylifebags[.]com[.]au) that hosts the EK's TDS location in a new browser tab when a user accesses the tainted website.

Another script present on a second domain (your-prizes-box[.]life) in the initial script requests the same redirect file that points to Spelevo exploit kit.


			

			 
			
			

			 
		The researchers speculate that the second domain may act as an extra tracker ensuring that the connection to the TDS is through the compromised website, indicating that a victim has fallen for the trap; a direct connection would signal that malware analysts are already on the trail.

After completing all the malicious redirects, the Spelevo campaign tries to hide redirects the signs of compromise with another redirect, this time to Google search page.

The effect of this final step is that "a user would see a tab open which loads the gate, followed by the landing page, an exploit page, and then finally to Google."

Exploit kits (EKs) have lost much of the might they had half a decade ago since the components they typically exploited (Internet Explorer, and browser plugins Adobe Flash and Java) started to benefit from a faster patching rate, have been pulled from browsers or are slowly being abandoned.

Despite losing their targets, exploit kits are still an efficient way to deliver payloads as they can achieve their goal without user intervention, in what is called a drive-by attack; the victim just has to visit a website hosting an exploit kit to get their computer infected.

The researchers say that with EKs, "all it takes is one missed patch on one system to lead directly to compromise."
