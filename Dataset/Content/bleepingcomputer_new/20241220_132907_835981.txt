TrickBot (also known as Trickster, TheTrick, and TrickLoader) is a banking Trojan that has been continuously upgraded throughout the years with new modules and capabilities since October 2016 when it was initially observed in the wild.

While in the beginning it only came with banking Trojan capabilities designed to collect and deliver as much sensitive data as possible to its masters, it is now also become a popular malware dropper capable of infecting compromised machines with other malware families.

Secureworks Counter Threat Unit (CTU) researchers were the ones who spotted this new TrickBot version during August 2019 after discovering new dynamic webinjects targeting U.S. mobile users' information.

New modules were added to target Verizon Wireless users on August 5, T-Mobile customers on August 12, and Sprint clients on August 19.

The webinjects allow the threat group behind the TrickBot botnet — dubbed GOLD BLACKBURN by Secureworks — to inject additional code within its victims' websites via web sessions manipulation.

"When a victim navigates to the website of one of these organizations, the legitimate server response is intercepted by TrickBot and proxied through a command and control (C2) server," explain the researchers.


TrickBot targeting Verizon users (the altered version on the left, original on the right)

The C2 server will then automatically inject HTML and JavaScript code within the victim's web browser causing "an additional form field that requests the user’s PIN code" to be added for customers of the three mobile carriers.

Besides injecting the extra PIN request form within its targets' login pages, this variant of TrickBot also injects code designed to capture and exfiltrate the harvested username, password, and PIN to its C2 server.

"These 'recordings' are presented to TrickBot operators as they browse through infected hosts in their web panel," as the Secureworks researchers found.

Going after mobile users' PIN codes hints at the threat actors' involvement or interest in operating a SIM swap fraud scheme (aka SIM splitting or Port-Out scam) which would allow them to take full control over their victim's phone numbers, "including all inbound and outbound text and voice communications."


PIN code attack targeting Sprint users

"The interception of short message service (SMS)-based authentication tokens or password resets is frequently used during account takeover (ATO) fraud," adds the report.

Organizations and users can switch from SMS-based multi-factor authentication (MFA) to time-based one-time password (TOTP) MFA and avoid using phone numbers as password reset options for high-risk accounts to mitigate this threat.

Indicators of compromise (IOCs) including IP addresses used by this TrickBot variant as dynamic webinjects proxy C2 servers are available at the end of Secureworks' report.

Regularly updated features and infection vectors

TrickBot is one of  if not the most frequently malware these days, with upgrades added to new versions spotted by security researchers on an almost weekly basis.

For instance, just in July 2019, the TrickBot Trojan was seen adding Windows Defender bypassing capabilities, getting a new IcedID proxy module to steal banking info, as well as a separate module for stealing browser cookies.

Further back, in January, researchers from CrowdStrike and FireEye found that TrickBot is being used as Access-as-a-Service by other actors to get access to previously infected networks. Once a machine was compromised and became a bot, the Trojan created reverse shells back to other actors, like the ones behind Ryuk, enabling them to further infiltrate the network and drop their payloads.

One year earlier, on March 2018, TrickBot was updated with a screenlocker component, suggesting at the time that its developers might start holding victims for ransom as a backup measure if banking info exfiltration was not possible.

Also, in July 2017, the banking Trojan became capable of self-propagation after being upgraded with a component designed to for automated self-spreading for boosting the chances of infecting as many machines with little to no extra effort, compromising entire networks at a time when and if possible.
