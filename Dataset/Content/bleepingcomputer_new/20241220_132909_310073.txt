At the 10th Usenix Workshop on Offensive Technologies, researchers Eric Wustrow from the University of Colorado Boulder and Benjamin VanderSloot from the University of Michigan unveiled DDoSCoin as a theoretical cryptocurrency that could prove to be more "efficient" than Bitcoin.

"DDoSCoin allows miners to select the victim servers by consensus using a proof-of-stake protocol. Rather than specify a single website or static list that DDoSCoin miners target, choosing them by consensus allows the choice of who is attacked to be made collectively and fairly by DDoSCoin participants."



Source: DDoSCoin: Cryptocurrency with a Malicious Proof-of-Work

DDoSCoin is a little bit different than Bitcoin. Miners of the latter cryptocurrency use computers to solve a number of complex mathematical puzzles. Those solutions produce a "proof-of-work," which makes fresh Bitcoins and demonstrates their legitimacy on a public record known as the blockchain.

By contrast, DDoS coin would function according to a malicious "Proof-of-DDoS" protocol that would accomplish a proof-of-work when a miner established a TLS connection with a website they're supposed to attack. Values shared in that connection would prove the miner had communicated with the target website's server.

In other words, that system would reward people with a reward in DDoSCoins when they prove they had attacked a target website with a sufficient number of distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack requests.



In the mind of Wustrow, something modeled like DDoSCoin could encourage hacktivists to commission others to launch DDoS attacks on their behalf. As quoted by Motherboard:


"...[I]t's probably still easier and more effective to just pay a 'reputable' botnet to do this for you. On the other hand, something similar to DDoSCoin might lower the barrier to collecting rewards for DoS attacks, ultimately driving down the cost for hacktivist consumers."


They could do so by using the system's proposed PAY_TO_DDOS feature. For example, if someone wanted to attack Australia's census website (which recently came under a DDoS attack), all they would need to do is set up a transaction containing a certain amount of DDoSCoins with two arguments: the target site's domain name and the desired number of attack requests. Someone could then complete that transaction by launching the specified attack and claiming the DDoSCoin reward posted in PAY_TO_DDOS transaction.

Who's to say a criminal organization or nation-state couldn't use such a system to launch DDoS attacks, all the while claiming plausible deniability?


Source: https://www.realnets.com/our-blog/massive-ddos-attacks-lizardstresser/

When it comes to those thorny ethnical questions, Wustrow didn't have much to say to Motherboard:


"I'm not a lawyer, so I won't speak about legal responsibility. In our paper we have only attempted to show that the idea is possible; we have not pushed to actually make it a reality today."


It probably does not require a lawyer to determine the legality of a coin like this. If someone was to implement this cryptocurrency, it could safely be said that encouraging people to perform Denial of Service Attacks for financial gain is illegal.
