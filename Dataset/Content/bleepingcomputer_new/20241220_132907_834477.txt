
	   


A new piece of software uses an unmodified USB device to leak data stored on an air-gapped computer over radio frequency (RF) signals.

Researchers Mordechai Guri, Matan Monitz, Yuval Elovici of Ben-Gurion University explain that their software, dubbed "USBee," creates electromagnetic waves from a USB device connected to the air-gapped computer. Those waves can be manipulated to contain sensitive information in the form of binary data.

"In this scenario, USBee software, installed on a compromised compute[r], uses a USB thumb drive already connected to the computer (Figure 1, A), and creates a short-range RF transmission modulated with data (e.g., passwords or encryption keys). The transmission can be received by a nearby receiver (Figure 1, B) where it is decoded and sent to an attacker."




USBee works because a standard USB consists of four shielded wires: VBUS, GND, D+, and D-. The researchers found that by using a technique called "bit stuffing" (manipulating the binary "0's" and "1's") with respect the D+ and D- wires, they could produce electromagnetic radiation (EMR) with a frequency between 240 Mhz and 480 Mhz.

The team could then use data modulation to alter the wave's frequency, with each change representing some distinct digital value.

That's essentially all it took. After generating the EMR, the researchers used a $30 RTL-SDR software-defined radio connected to a laptop to receive the transmission, at which point they were then able to make the data readable at a rate of 80 bytes / sec.



This isn't the first piece of malware that's sought to compromise an air-gapped system. In 2014, another research team at Ben-Gurion designed "AirHopper," a proof-of-concept malware which broadcasts data from an air-gapped computer's video display unit. A nearby actor can, in turn, collect that transmission using their mobile phone.

Neither can we forget the COTTONMOUTH tool. Disclosed in the Edward Snowden leaks, COTTONMOUTH is a USB dongle modified with an RF transmitter and receiver that is also capable of bridging an air gap.



USBee is unlike and AirHopper and COTTONMOUTH, however, in that it makes use of an unmodified USB device. (AirHopper doesn't use USB, whereas COTTONMOUTH requires a USB device to be outfitted with special RF technology.)

There's just one catch: an air-gapped computer first needs to be infected with malware in order for USBee to work. And if the computer isn't connected to the web, that could prove to be quite difficult. Still, USB-borne malicious programs like Stuxnet prove it's not impossible.

With that being said, perhaps USBee could figure prominently in some state sponsored actor's attack toolkit sometime in the not-too-distant future.
