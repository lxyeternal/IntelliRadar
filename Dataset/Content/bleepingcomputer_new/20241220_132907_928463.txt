
	   


Researchers discovered a new phishing campaign that abuses QR codes to redirect targets to phishing landing pages, effectively circumventing security solutions and controls designed to stop such attacks in their tracks.

The crooks behind the phishing attacks which targeted French Cofense customers used a URL encoded in a QR code to circumvent security software which analyzes and blocks suspicious or blacklisted domains.

The phishing emails were camouflage as a SharePoint email with a "Review Important Document" subject line and a message body which would invite potential victims "Scan Bar Code To View Document."

Phishing email

To lure the victims to their phishing landing pages, the crooks added a GIF image containing the QR code which would redirect them to the hxxps://digitizeyourart.whitmers[.]com/wp-content/plugins/wp-college/Sharepoint/sharepoint/index.php domain designed to pose as a SharePoint-related site.

"Most smartphone QR code scanner apps will instantly redirect the user to the malicious website via the phone’s native browser," as the Cofense researchers found out.

After reaching a SharePoint branded phishing landing page on their smartphones, the targets are presented with the option to login using AOL, Microsoft, or another type of account of their choice to view the document.

However, as Cofense adds, "While this sounds like a simple phish, there is a more nefarious tactic in play: removing the user from the security of a corporate business network."


Phishing landing page

Removing their victims from the relative safety of their computers allows the cybercriminals to effectively bypass any link protection services, secure email gateways, sandboxes, or web content filters set in place by the targets' corporate information security department.

This happens because the phishing landing pages are loaded on the victim's personal smartphone or, in the more fortunate cases, on a company-provided mobile device which might still expose them to extra risks because of more limited security solutions and controls being implemented on a corporate level — if any.

To make the attack even more successful against mobile users, the attackers have also optimized their landing pages for smartphones with the phishing page providing a custom view on mobile devices.


Phishing landing page on mobile

"Though the user may now be using their personal device to access the phish, they are still in the 'corporate' mindset as the original email was received at their business email address," as Cofense further highlights.

"Therefore, it is highly likely that the victim would input their corporate account credentials to attempt to access this 'document'."

The QR code-powered phishing emails successfully reached the inboxes of Cofense customers in corporate environments where Symantec Messaging Gateway was running, with the messages being tagged as "Not spam" by the security solution.

An almost identical phishing campaign was detected by VadeSecure during the summer of 2016, hosted on a hacked WordPress site, targeting French victims, and seen bypassing "the vast majority of phishing, virus, spam, and malware filters because there is no obvious URL for the filters to examine for legitimacy."

QRishing is a tried-and-true, still viable attack method

This is a well-known technique used by cybercriminals to avoid phishing filters and security solutions built to block such attacks before the malicious emails reach the targets' inboxes.

Dubbed QRishing, it was described in a Carnegie Mellon University’s CyLab study from 2012 titled “QRishing: The Susceptibility of Smartphone Users to QR Code Phishing Attacks.”

The study concluded that "The ease with which such an attack can be mounted against current smartphones is particularly concerning given the long patching cycle and potential for an attacker to gain elevated privileges on the device."

A paper from 2017 also proposed a possible defense against them named QRCS (Quick Response Code Secure), which would be "a universal efficient and effective solution focusing exclusively on the authenticity of the originator and consequently the integrity of QR code by using digital signatures."

Unfortunately, as Cofense discovered, crooks are still employing QRishing to target their victims, avoiding any phishing security controls set up on their computers by migrating the attack to less-protected mobile devices.
