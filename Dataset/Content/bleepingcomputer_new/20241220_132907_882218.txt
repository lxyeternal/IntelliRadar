
	   


A recent targeted attack against organizations in the satellite and communications industry echoes techniques seen in campaigns from cyberespionage group MuddyWater.

The attack leveraged the recently reported 19-year old vulnerability (CVE-2018-20250) in WinRAR (now patched) to launch a convoluted infection chain in an attempt to run a fileless PowerShell backdoor. Successful compromise could grant the adversary full control of the target machine.

With over 100 distinct exploits  emerging in the first week after disclosure in February, the WinRAR vulnerability was not an opportunity to be missed, even by the more sophisticated cyberespionage groups.

Noticing an aattack with a higher sophistication level, the Office 365 ATP Research Team started to analyze the incident.

Complex chain of events

Rex Plantado of Microsoft's Office 365 ATP Research Team says that the compromise attempt started with a phishing email pretending to be from the Ministry of Foreign Affairs in Afghanistan.



The message had a Word document attached and asked the recipient for satellite image resources but did not contain hidden malicious code, most likely to evade detection of email security products.

However, the document did not contain any information pertaining to the request; instead, it directed the potential victim to download a new document from a provided link that pointed to storage in OneDrive.



The second Word document is the payload for the initial stage of the attack. It contains a highly obfuscated macro. By default, products in Microsoft Office suite come with support for macros disabled.

"Enabling the macro starts a series of malicious actions that leads to the download of the malware payload," which requires a computer restart to run, says Plantado.

Conveniently, a "Next" button is embedded in the malicious document taking the victim to a fake message that prompts for a reboot because a specific DLL is not present on the system.



A long road to delivering a malicious archive

In the background, the malicious macro runs a series of actions designed to obtain a second-stage PowerShell script brimming with multi-layered encryption. According to the researcher, the script is similar to code used in previous MuddyWater campaigns.

"The second-stage PowerShell script collects system information, generates unique computer ID, and sends these to a remote location," explains Plantado. 

Its purpose is that of a backdoor, but it can also download arbitrary files, run commands via Command Prompt, and decode a base64-encoded command and run it using PowerShell.

It is at this stage that the vulnerability in WinRAR pays off. Because the script can download and execute files, the attacker can now deliver a malicious archive and exploit CVE-2018-20250 to take control of the station. This happens when the victim opens the crafted archive.

MuddyWater, also known as SeedWorm, is a relatively new adversary, first observed in 2017 and it was seen targeting organizations in Afghanistan in the past, as well as entities connected to the Middle East.
