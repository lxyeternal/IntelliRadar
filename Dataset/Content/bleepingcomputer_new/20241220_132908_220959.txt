
	   


Financial and social security identity theft services are becoming more and more affordable every year on the dark web, leading to a drop in the skill level required for tax fraud schemes.

This means that even the most inexperienced cybercriminals can now quickly whip up a whole new identity with just a couple of mouse clicks, without even having to bother making a call or meeting with an identity fraud "provider" face to face.

More to the point, it is just as easy for a crook to get his hands on the documents and credentials needed for running a successful tax identity theft campaign these days as is for someone to order a pizza using a food delivery service.

			Forged W-2s and tax refund cashing out guide
			
		This shows that the cybercriminals who are selling this information and the crooks who buy it aren't just going to steal their victims' money but they're also into the business of ruining their financial future.

In addition, Carbon Black found that "For a more comprehensive investment (around $1,000) a relatively inexperienced hacker can purchase authenticated access to a U.S.-based bank account, file a false tax return, claim the IRS refund and cash out via a cryptocurrency exchange for a 100+% return on investment."

The researchers also noticed that products and services related to tax identity theft crimes are also getting cheaper and cheaper on dark markets because the sellers are increasingly more numerous and the competition between them has noticeably intensified.

Dark web markets also overflow with "listings selling 'how-to' guides for cashing out with tax returns. These listings are cheap, some as low as $5, but do not provide any actual tax data. The sellers that offer these listings usually list other similar documentation for credit card fraud and PayPal scams."


IRS Reports 2017 vs 2018

The fact that the data needed for tax identity theft is so easy to come also made the U.S. government crack down on tax fraud, a fact highlighted by comparing the IRS criminal investigation annual reports for the last two years. [2017, 2018]

For instance, while in 2017 the total amount of identified tax fraud was of $2,5 billion, in 2018 it quadrupled reaching a record-breaking $9,69 billion.

The proceeds identified for other financial crimes have also skyrocketed during last year, increasing from $1,1 billion during 2017 to a startling $10,4 billion in 2018.

"On its website, the IRS outlines its 'Dirty Dozen' tax scams, highlighting some techniques well known by security professionals, with phishing topping the list," says Carbon Black. However, although identity theft is also on the list, "the role the dark web plays in this theft is glaringly absent."

As the researchers further discovered, even the lowest skilled cybercriminal who wants to get a tax fraud "career" can do it with just a mere $1000 investment on the dark web market which would allow him to buy credentials for a U.S. Bank account, file false tax returns, get refunds from the IRS, move the funds into a crypto wallet, exchange the cryptocurrency through multiple transactions on the dark web, and then just cash everything out using a bank account from another continent


Tax fraud cycle

Following this simple fraud cycle will essentially provide the crook with an almost 100% return on the initial investment, making it a very attractive occupation for people already looking into making some illicit investments.

"The United States tax refund system, when exposed to the ruthless efficiency of dark web marketplaces, has been turned into a Vegas-style slot machine. Insert some bitcoin, pull the handle and figure out how to receive your $2,000 - $3,000 from the U.S. Treasury courtesy of a faceless victim thousands of miles away," concluded the Carbon Black research team.

In related news, just two days ago, over 60,000 stolen digital profiles were found up for sale on Genesis Store, a private and invite-only cybercriminal market discovered by Kaspersky Lab's researchers.

Also, on April 5, Cisco Talos' security researcher published a report exposing an online black market which offered cybercrime goods and services on Facebook, a dark market spread over 74 Facebook groups and totaling around 385,000 members.
