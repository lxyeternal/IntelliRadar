This new attack poses a serious security threat because most devices inherently trust all the data coming from accelerometers and don't feature protection systems to detect spoofed signals coming from local sensors.

Sound waves induce fake motion into accelerometers

The principle behind how the attack works is simple. Accelerometers have a sensing mass that sits on a series of springs. By sending finely-tuned sound waves to the accelerometer, the sound waves induce controlled movement into sensing mass.

In a series of examples documented in a research paper released this week, researchers showed how they used specially-crafted sound waves to attack accelerometers inside phones and tablets, and Fitbit fitness monitors.



Researchers used sound waves aimed at a phone's accelerometer to interact with software on the device that controlled a remote control car. By using previously prepared sound waves, researchers made the car move without interacting with the smartphone.

Similarly, they also used sound waves aimed at a Fitbit device to record 3,000 steps on the device, generating fake data and earning financial rewards.

Attack has widespread repercussions

The attacks were trivial and had no immediate effect on the security of the device's human operator. Nevertheless, their research shows a new attack vector that can be truly devastating if used for nefarious purposes, and against devices in industrial systems, where accelerometers are often deployed.

Researchers also said they successfully tested their attack on 20 different accelerometers from five different vendors.

Scientists say the sound waves used in these attacks could be embedded in songs played over a user's speakers. No high-end speakers are needed, and regular $5 speakers could be used to replay the malicious sound waves.

Of the 20 models of accelerometers we tested, our experiments show that 75% are vulnerable to output biasing attacks (i.e., insecure low pass filters enable false fluctuating output measurements under acoustic interference), and 65% are vulnerable to output control attacks (i.e., insecure amplifiers enable false constant output measurements under acoustic interference). [...] The results confirm our concerns that system software does not adequately validate the  integrity of sensory data — blindly trusting the output of sensors by default.

The technical details of the attack are described in a paper titled "WALNUT: Waging Doubt on the Integrity of MEMS Accelerometers with Acoustic Injection Attacks," authored by four researchers from the University of Michigan, and one from the University of South Carolina.
