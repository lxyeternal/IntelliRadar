
	   


Fraudsters taking advantage of the legitimate 'free money' campaigns for the Cash App peer-to-peer payment service target users Twitter, Instagram, and YouTube with fake promises of financial gains.

The scams are simple yet effective, tricking Cash App users into willingly sending small amounts of money with a pledge of returning sums sometimes even ten times higher.

Riding the #CashApp hashtag

Square's Cash App payment service rose to huge popularity lately through marketing campaigns that offer small cash rewards, typically of $5 or $10, to users engaging with the brand on social media platforms like Twitter or Instagram.

Larger giveaways under the hashtags #CashAppFriday and #SuperCashAppFriday with total rewards between $10,000 and $75,000 have been running since 2017, sending $100 to $500 into the accounts of random Cash App users.

To be eligible, users only have to bring friends or enemies to the platform.



Fraudsters took notice of the campaigns and started their own game, promising huge amounts of money to people that spread their word, using the same hashtag in the legitimate promotions.

The trick here is to convince potential victims to send a smaller amount of money, guaranteeing a much higher return. To instill trust, they even post images with transactions allegedly from winners.



A keen eye will notice in the picture above that the transferred amount is not consistent with the one advertised and that the transaction is to a bank account (most likely the scammer's), not a Cash App user.

Satnam Narang of cybersecurity company Tenable found that some fraudsters claim to have software that can change or 'flip' the amounts in a transaction or that they are Cash App representatives that can make such a modification.

Users contacting the fraudster learn that they have to make a deposit first, which can range between $10 to $1,000. To make the deal sweet for both sides, the scammer only asks for a cut for their services.



In rare cases, some victims are offered proof that the trick works by turning a $2 deposit into a $20 return. This is just a small investment to make the victim deposit a larger amount.

Prepaid or gift cards are also an accepted form of payment. It goes without saying that victims falling for the trick never get any money back or a reply from the scammer.



Narang found that other methods to monetize from Cash App's popularity exist. For instance, asking the victim to sign up for services that offer money in return; Dosh Cash and Waldo, which give $5 per referral.

By convincing other users to register with a provided referral code, the scammer caches in from the service and never delivers on the promise of a Cash App transfer.



Impersonating the Cash App service or celebrities are other methods the fraudsters use to gain users' trust. Sometimes, victims are sent a request to make a deposit, for "verification" purposes, before they get the higher prize.

Finding the Cash App usernames to target is easy since the legitimate promotions ask participants to leave this detail in the comments on Instagram and replies on Twitter.

Square and Twitter CEO Jack Dorsey is among the personalities used for this type of fraud. 



Phishing is another risk associated with these fake Cash App promotions as some cybercriminals send out messages claiming a money prize.

Also included is a shortened link to a web page that asks for the login credentials for the service. In such cases, two-factor authentication is a good way to prevent an account takeover.

Some links noticed by Narang had as many as 500 clicks, most of them from users in the U.S. and the United Kingdom.



A different method on YouTube

Scammers take their business on all popular social platforms and YouTube is on the fave list. However, the method is different here.

Instead of running false Cash App campaigns, Google's video platform is used to promote 'money generators' or 'cash app hacks.' Using these lures, the scammers' video shows how they add money to an empty Cash App account.

To do the same, they instruct viewers to go to a certain website that purportedly offers a top-up service where users can choose an amount to deliver to their account. However, the process is not straightforward and users are required to verify their human status.



Then they are redirected to websites that ask them to install mobile games and play for a specific amount of time. These steps will allegedly add the funds. What viewers don't realize is that the video was doctored to show profit in the scammer's account.

The fraudster's profit is likely in the affiliate money earned from users installing the promoted apps. Even if the fee is small per installation, the money adds up when many users are convinced to download the apps.

Narang's advice is to proceed with caution when it comes to free money. Even if some promotions are legitimate, it never hurts to check whose behind the campaign. Some things to keep in mind:

Cash App or artists giving away money don't ask for a deposit, for verification purposes
	If a promotion offers huge amounts of money in rewards, it is probably a scam; total value of prizes in legitimate campaigns is typically kept to normal amounts. A $75,000 weekly promotion is normal, costs close to $1 million are suspicious
	There is no method to increase a deposited amount tenfold; if such proof is offered for a low amount, it's probably worth the risk (you can scam the scammer and never reply when they ask for a larger deposit)
	Winner notifications that include a link to a page asking you to log in or for sensitive info (phone number) are likely phishing attempts
	Hacks and Cash App generators that produce money out of thin air are as real as that Nigerian prince that sits on a fortune and needs your help to move the money
Cash App provides settings to avoid getting scammed. "Security Lock" asks for a PIN before making a transfer and "Contacts Only" allows requests only from people in your list of contacts.
