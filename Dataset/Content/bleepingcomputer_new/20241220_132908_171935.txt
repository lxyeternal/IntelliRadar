
	   


A ransomware attack hit a remote data backup service and encrypted files from dental practices in the U.S. Hundreds of customers relying on the backup solution had their data locked by the Sodinokibi file-encrypting malware.

The incident happened on August 26 and impacted businesses using  DDS Safe, an online backup product from Digital Dental Record (DDS), which got infected through its cloud management provider, PercSoft.

Data restoration in progress

On Tuesday morning, a joint statement from the two companies handling the medical record backups informed that a decryptor would be available and the fix would take between 30 minutes and four hours. A later update announced that data restoration had started.

"Our priority is to get the practice management up and you able to take xrays." - PercSoft


One may think that being a data backup service, additional safe copies would allow the recovery.

But in this case restoration was possible because the ransomware operators were paid for the decryption tool. Brian Krebs published on his blog a screenshot "purportedly from a conversation between PerCSoft and an affected dental office" where the company allegedly says that they were paying the ransom.


credit: Brian Krebs

The money demand has not been disclosed publicly or in private forums, but some users believe that PercSoft said that it was $5,000 per client. It is also said that 500 customers were affected, which would amount to a total payment of $2.5 million. This info may not be completely reliable, though.

Paying the attacker for a decryption key cannot be avoided in lack of a healthy data backups, but sometimes the decryption cost can be lowered significantly. A reverse engineer analyzing the malware could find a flaw that may allow deriving keys to restore files on multiple infected systems.

Fabian Wosar of Emsisoft says that in some cases a workaround is available that could have significantly reduced the cost for victims. In order to prevent the developers from fixing the potential loophole, we will not disclose it publicly.

Krebs learned from Brenna Sadler, director of communications for the Wisconsin Dental Association, that the ransomware locked data of about 400 dental practices, and that for up to 100 of them the records were restored.


credit: Brian Krebs

The journalist also learned that the ransomware strain wreaking havoc is Sodinokibi, also known as REvil.

Although it emerged in April, Sodinokibi has made quite a reputation and is currently one of the most active ransomware families. Unlike most malware of the same type, Sodinokibi does not rely on phishing alone for distribution and also exploits vulnerabilities to infect systems.
