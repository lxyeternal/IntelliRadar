
	   


A huge customer database containing 11 million records that include personal details, has been discovered on Monday sitting online, unprotected.

The data was available from a MongoDB instance set up on the hosting infrastructure from Grupo-SMS USA, LLC, and could be accessed by anyone able to find the path to it.

Independent security researcher Bob Diachenko found the information by scanning the internet using publicly available tools. His research revealed that the dataset had been last indexed by Shodan search engine on September 13, but it is unclear how long it was open for access before that date.

It also holds names (first and last), physical addresses, ZIP code, and customers' state and city of residence.



This information alone is a boon for criminals running all sorts of illegal or shady businesses: spammers, scammers (tech support, tax), botnet herders, or malware peddlers of any kind (ransomware, cryptomining, spyware, info stealers, banking).

Ownership of the database: unknown

A sample from the database shared by Diachenko shows that some records may be from users of SaverSpy website, which provides printable and digital discount coupons for a wide range of products. SaverSpy was created about a month ago, on August 13, and it is part of an affiliate program that provides coupon offers from Coupons.com, a Quotient Technology website.



We asked Quotient about the database, and a representative of the company told BleepingComputer that the data "did not originate with Quotient Technology; there was no breach on our end."

The steps for getting the discount coupons from both SaverSpy and Coupons websites are exactly the same. Neither require registration to get the goods, and they simply list an offer and let users pick what they need. If the user's computer or mobile phone is connected to a printer, they are asked to do an SMS verification on a phone number they can access.

The alternative is to deliver the coupon to an email address the user chooses, so they can print from any computer they want.

According to SaverSpy's privacy policy page, the website collects the following user information: name, address, phone number, e-mail address, and other demographic information, upon registration for an account. We could not find the account signup section, but there is the option to subscribe to offers via email.

In a conversation with BleepingComputer, Diachenko said that the samples he analyzed had no phone numbers or payment card details. He told us that apart from customer personal information, the database also included some details about the email correspondence status with the users, that showed if the email went through and the campaign it promoted.

Even if the ownership of the database remains a mystery, the information is no longer exposed at the time of publishing.

Database unreachable, but others had access to it

In a blog post today, the researcher writes that the database was tagged as 'Compromised' in Shodan, and contained a ransom note demanding its owners to pay 0.4 BTC.

Since the information was intact, one can assume that the takeover attempt was the result of an automated process and the scripts failed to work properly. This is not uncommon.



Diachenko tried to contact Quotient Technologies about the potential data breach but received no answer.
