
	   


Hackers have revealed details about the inner workings of a cyber-espionage group mostly known in the security community as OilRig, APT34, and HelixKitten, linked to the Iranian government.

Using the online name Lab Dookhtegan, the hackers used a Telegram channel to dump information about APT34's infrastructure, hacking tools, members, and victims.

The data dump is no hoax. We received confirmation from Chronicle, Alphabet's cybersecurity company, that the leaked information is associated with the APT34/OilRig group.

If more than one individual did this, it is not a large group, someone familiar with the situation in Iran told us.

Brandon Levene, Head of Applied Intelligence at Chronicle, believes that one reason behind the leak would be to put Iranian cyber capabilities in the limelight; another would be to disrupt future operation by forcing APT34 to retool.

Dookhtegan did not hide their feelings toward the government's cyber activity, expressing hope that other Iranians will do something to fight the regime.


"We are exposing here the cyber tools (APT34 / OILRIG) that the ruthless Iranian Ministry of Intelligence has been using against Iran’s neighboring countries, including names of the cruel managers, and information about the activities and the goals of these cyber-attacks.  We hope that other Iranian citizens will act for exposing this regime’s real ugly face!"


Dookhtegan also leaked the names and phone numbers of some individuals working for the Iranian Ministry of Intelligence (data on its employees is classified) as well as pictures, names, phone numbers, and email addresses of alleged hackers in the OilRig group.

In an update minutes ago, Dookhtegan said that personal information about the staff working at the Ministry of Intelligence will trickle every few days. About ten minutes later the channel was aupdated with new info (picture, name, and phone number) about another alleged member of the OilRig group.



Tools and infrastructure

The leaks started on March 26 and comprise source code for home-grown tools, URLs to web shells on servers from organizations all over the world, governments included, web shell access details, usernames and passwords from compromised targets.

In a tweet yesterday, Kaspersky threat researcher Alexey Firsch published a sample of the 117 web shell URLs Dookhtegan made public:



In total, Dookhtegan published code for six tools used in OilRig operations: Poison Frog and Glimpse PowerShell-based backdoors (both versions of a tool called BondUpdater according to Palo Alto Networks), web shells HyperShell and HighShell, Fox Panel, and Webmask (the DNSpionage tool analyzed by Cisco Talos).

In an analysis from freelance security researcher MisterCh0c, Poison Frog's server part is incomplete and cannot be used as it is.



The researcher also checked the two web shells and found that in the case of HyperShell a cookie named 'p' and a correct password is required to get access. MisterCh0c says that Dookhtegan changed "all of the most meaningful passwords" with 'Th!sN0tF0rFAN'.

OilRig compromised targets

Dookhtegan also published an impressive set of details about the victims of OilRig. These are mainly entities (government agencies and companies) from the Middle East: Dubai Media Inc, Etihad Airways, Abu Dhabi Airports , Emirates National Oil, Lamprell Energy Ltd., Amiri Diwan of Kuwait, Oman Administrative Court, Emirates Prime Minister Office, National Security Agency of Bahrain.

The data accompanying the dumps consisted of URLs for the OilRig shells as well as login credentials, thousands of them.

In the dump for the Emirates Ministry of Presidential Affairs (mopa.ae) there are about 900 usernames and passwords and more than 80 for webmail access.

The archive for Dubai Media has more than 250 credential sets. The one for Etihad Airways has over 10,000 usernames and plain text password, apparently cracked with the 'advanced edition' of a tool called Windows Password Recovery. Other files include server info, database dumps, and output data from Mimikatz  post-exploitation tool.

The trove of data was pulled from the command and control servers used by OilRig, Dookhtegan says, backing the claim with pictures of control panels and proof of their infiltration.





Chronicle's Levene thinks that the leak could be a blow to APT34's operations as future activity needs rethinking.

"It’s likely this group will alter their toolset in order to maintain operational status,” Levene comments, adding that this also depends on how much the group cares about being burned.

"There may be some copycat activity derived from the leaked tools, but it is unlikely to see widespread use," Levene says. Poison Frog and Glimpse ahve code that can be modified to run command and control operations, but most "most of the leaked tools are in some way hamstrung," the researcher added.
