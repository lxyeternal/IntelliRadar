The first report named Italy as the origin for the sample upload to VirusTotal, while a new detection of a different strain of the malware was noted on the same scanning platform three days later, on December 13, from the Netherlands.

Different trigger date, still in the past

About 80% of this new sample of Shamoon, aka Disstrack, is similar to earlier versions of the malware. Some contrasts are noticeable, though.

Among the differences, analysts at Anomali Labs say, is the trigger date still set in the past but to December 12, 2017, five days later than the one set in the variant identified earlier.

A trigger or detonation date is typically set to activate the malware. In these case, it's not clear why the threat actor used dates in the past. Ghareeb Saad, Threat Intelligence Manager at Anomali, does not exclude the possibility of a mistake.

He told BleepingComputer that Shamoon can retrieve detonation dates from its command and control (C2) server; the samples examined by Anomali Labs did not have the C2 configured.

A third explanation for the historic trigger date is that the adversary wanted Shamoon to become active immediately after reaching the target.

"This may be achieved by altering the detonation date to 1 year in the past. Therefore, it is possible that a sample with a detonation date of December 12, 2017, represents a second wave of Shamoon V3 malware that was utilized on December 12, 2018," Anomali Labs' researchers reason.

Basic attempts to pass unnoticed

Another deviation is the use of the UPX (Ultimate Packer for eXecutable), which was not disclosed with sample discovered on December 10. 

Packing an executable modifies the signature and leads to bypassing signature-based detection, so this choice may be a method to keep a low profile.

Since 80% of the malware is recognizable, using UPX seems like a tactical maneuver from the threat actor.

Furthermore, the new Shamoon uses "VMWare Workstation" in its file description, probably in an attempt to pass as a VMWare setup executable during a superficial check.

Saad told us that there isn't sufficient evidence to confirm this goal, although "this tactic is commonly used with social engineering attacks or to prevent users from performing a basic investigation of the file."



Author changes resource names

Previous versions of Shamoon in 2012 and 2016 came with a set of resource files that were used to replace destroyed files. In the sample analyzed by Chronicle, the binary section that typically contained the resource is empty. 

This is not the case with the variant studied by Saad, which includes three image files named 'GNL', 'PICNIC', and 'UNM,' as shown below.



"The files appear to have Arabic Yemen language property," the researcher notes.

Despite the presence of these files, the malware does not use them at all. Instead, the same ElDos RawDisk driver is employed to destroy storage by overwriting the master boot record partitions with random data, as observed by Palo Alto Network in their analysis of the first Shamoon sample this month.

The initial infection vector for the Shamoon strains discovered this month remains unknown, just like the person that uploaded them to the VirusTotal scanning platform.

Last week, oil services provider Saipem disclosed that over 300 of its servers had been impacted by Shamoon.

With this second sample, there is no victim attribution because no company reported a data-wiping cyber attack and the researchers did not find clues that that pointed to a target.

At the moment of writing, both variants of the data wiper are widely detected by antivirus engines on VirusTotal.
