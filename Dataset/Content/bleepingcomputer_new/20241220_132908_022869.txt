
	   


A phishing campaign dropping the Qbot banking Trojan with the help of delivery emails camouflaging as parts of previous conversations was spotted during late March 2019 by the JASK Special Operations team.

Qbot (also known as QakBot and Pinkslipbot) is a quite old yet still active and continuously evolving banking Trojan with worm capabilities, used by malicious actors since at least 2009 [1, 2, 3, 4] to steal financial data and banking credentials from their targets, to drop additional malware, to log user keystrokes,  and create a backdoor to compromised machines.

As detailed by the JASK SpecOps security researchers, "The delivery mechanism for this Qbot infection was a phishing campaign where the targeted user received an email containing a link to an online document. Interestingly enough, the delivery email was actually a reply to a pre-existing email thread."

Phishing e-mail

The Qbot payload will be downloaded on the already compromised machine with the help of the legitimate Windows BITSAdmin utility (bitsadmin.exe) in the form of an 'August.png' file (with 53 hits on VirusTotal), which according to the JASK SpecOps team is "a known stage two download associated with Qbot banking malware."

As discovered by the security research team at Varonis while analyzing another Qbot campaign during early March, the payload will gain persistence by copying "itself to %Appdata%\Roaming\{Randomized String}" and creating a registry value to have its loader launched when the user logs on, a scheduled task to relaunch itself every 5 hours, as well as a shortcut in the startup folder for good measure.


Downloading the Qbot malware payload

Subsequently, the malware loader will inject itself into an explorer.exe process and will also overwrite its own executable with a 32-bit calc.exe version.

After settling in on the compromised host, the Qbot malware will start brute forcing network accounts for lateral movement purposes using a list of local account credentials extracted by Varonis and available HERE.

As further explained by the Varonis research team, Qbot will then employ various techniques to steal as much financial data as possible from its victims, which gets sent to its masters' command-and-control servers. 

Some of the techniques Qbot uses to steal sensitive information:


Keylogging – Qbot captures and sends every keystroke that the victim enters and uploads them to the attacker.
	Credentials/cookies – Qbot searches for saved credentials/cookies from browsers and sends them to the attacker.
	Hooking – the main payload injects to all the processes in the system with a code that hooks API calls and searches for financial/banking string the malware extracts the data, credentials, or session cookies from the process and uploads it to the attacker.


Dropping malware context-aware phishing is not a novel method as it goes as far back as 2017 when Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 detected a spear phishing campaign which used this method to leverage the CVE-2017-0199 Microsoft Word Office/WordPad Remote Code Execution Vulnerability.

One year later, the Gozi ISFB banking trojan was also distributed as the malware payload using this same technique from compromised devices part of the Dark Cloud botnet, with the attackers delivering phishing emails that appeared to be part of existing email threads for some extra stealthiness as discovered by Cisco Talos in March 2018.

During October 2018, Trend Micro also unearthed a phishing campaign which made use of hijacked e-mail accounts to drop the URSNIF information-stealing Trojan  also camouflaged as a part of an already active e-mail conversation.

The hacking group behind Emotet also successfully employed this technique (and spread Qbot as a second stage malware payload) as reported by Minerva Labs on March 11, with the phishing campaign really picking up speed during April as noticed by the Cofense and Kryptos Logic security companies, as well as by security researcher Marcus Hutchins (aka MalwareTech).
