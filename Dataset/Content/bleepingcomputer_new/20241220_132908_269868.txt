
	   


Even though credential stuffing is a popular method used by hacking groups to attack businesses since at least late 2014, there still is a lot to be uncovered about the techniques malicious actors use to run them.

Credential stuffing attacks consist of automated attempts to log into online services using user credential databases, with the success of this type of malicious campaigns being based on the unfortunately very common habit of users to use the same password for multiple accounts.

Credential collections and stuffing attacks

Recorded Future shed some light on the subject in an extensive report which shows how cybercriminals have been able to employ sets of millions of credentials stolen from high profile online platforms and companies such as Adobe, Dailymotion, VK, Bell, Tumblr, Comcast, Fling, Sony, and LinkedIn, as well as huge collections of hundreds of millions of stolen accounts sold on the dark web marketplaces [1, 2] to run credential stuffing attacks.

Since the beginning of 2019 there have already been a number of successful campaigns which managed to infiltrate the computing systems of TurboTax, Dunkin' Donuts, Basecamp, and Dailymotion.

Additionally, an Akamai report from February showed that approximately 28 billion credential stuffing attempts were detected between May and December 2018, retail websites being the main targets of credential abuse with 10 billion attempts.

Credential stuffing toolset

As further explained by Recorded Future, apart from large stolen or leaked credential collections, threat actors also have to get their hands on dedicated account-checking tools and a pool of proxies to choose from and use during the attacks to avoid having their IP addresses blacklisted.

While sounding intimidating for an outsider, for cybercriminals who use darkmarkets on a daily basis buying a database of tens or hundreds of thousands of proxies is just as easy as filling up one's gas tank.

"With an investment of as little as $550, criminals could expect to earn at least 20 times the profit on the sale of compromised login credentials," also says the report. For the $550, the crooks can get a $150 checking software, a leaked/stolen credentials database of roughly 100K user records, and enough proxies to run their credential attacks for $250/week.

Recorded Future also explains that "for every one million random combinations of emails and passwords, attackers can potentially compromise between 10,000 and 30,000 accounts. Moreover, the same database could then be reused over and over again to hack dozens of different websites, yielding even higher profits."



Things are just as simple when it comes to getting their hands on an account-checking software designed to automate the process of logging in using stolen credentials, given that at least six of them are actively advertised according to Recorded Future's report.

More to the point, while there's at least a free tool (marketed as donationware) named STORM, crooks can also get their hands on paid login brute-forcing software such as Black Bullet, Private Keeper, SNIPR, Sentry MBA, and WOXY, which come with price tags ranging from $0.80 to $20.

Each of them comes with its own set of features, from captcha bypass capabilities, proxy support with auto-updating lists, and Selenium Webdriver support to concurrent sessions and dictionary-based brute-forcing.

Credential stuffing mitigation

What's certain for the time being is that there is and will not be a shortage of massive collections of credentials such as the 87.18 GB Collection #1 one discovered during mid-January to be used by hacking groups in credential stuffing attacks.

Organizations can protect their users by implementing multi-factor authentication (MFA) "which blocks the credential stuffing attack vector" but some of them already confirmed that they "may not be prepared to choose security over convenience."

On the other hand, end users can do their part by never using the same password on more than one service, utilizing a password manager to safely store their credentials and generate more complex passwords, as well as turning on two-factor authentication (2FA) for all services that support it.

Users can also subscribe to breach notification services such as Troy Hunt's highly useful haveibeenpwned.com platform to be informed when one of their accounts was in credential database leaked following a security breach.

Recorded Future recommends a couple of other mitigation measures organizations can take to prevent and block credential stuffing attempts:

• Criminals will often use paid proxy services aside from using publicly available free proxies to further obfuscate attacks. However, our analysis shows that such services often use geo-spoofing techniques to create a wide pool of IPs. Such domains will have the same IP addresses, but they will use different subnets. Monitoring for web traffic activity from such IPs offers additional mitigation capabilities.
• Monitoring criminal underground communities for the availability of new configuration files targeting your organization, acquisition, and the thorough analysis of such files for additional attack indicators.
