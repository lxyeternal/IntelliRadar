The interference was part of an experiment meant to highlight the risk of using mobile phone networks to authenticate and interact on the social networking service.

Simple trick with old technology

Getting privileges to post on the timeline of the selected accounts was possible because Twitter offers the option to tweet as long as it is done from the phone number connected to the profile.

Phone numbers can be spoofed and mobile text messages can be intercepted.

SIM-swapping is one way to steal someone's phone number and use it to send out text messages. More complex methods exist to achieve the same effect, such as the "Ghost Telephonist," demonstrated by researchers in Qihoo 360's UnicornTeam in 2017 at DEF CON and BlackHat security conferences.

Mike Godfrey, chief executive at security firm Insinia Security, said that simply spoofing someone's number would be enough to gain permission to post on their Twitter account.

Talking to BleepingComputer, he said that this trick was far from being sophisticated from a technical perspective. "It's a simple attack using old technology," he told us.

Twitter over SMS is the weak spot

If your mobile carrier supports the feature, you can get text messages from Twitter and even use the service over SMS. You can use the text service to send commands to follow users, like tweets, enable/disable notifications, or communicate via Twitter direct messages. Since validating the sender of a text message is not possible, Twitter will execute the orders received from the phone number linked to the user's account.

"If we can text from what appears to be your number then we can interact with, and fully control, your Twitter account," Insinia wrote in a post describing the experiment.

Once they got the "victim's" phone numbers, they spoofed the text messages so they appeared to come from the legitimate user.

"We used this method to successfully control the targets Twitter account, allowing us to send Tweets, DM’s, retweet and like tweets, follow and unfollow people and much more!"

Using this method they were able to publish on their targets' tweet stream the message "This account has been temporarily hijacked by INSINIA SECURITY."

Verified accounts of Simon Calder (45.3K followers, journalist for The Independent), Eamonn Holmes (983K followers, TV presenter and radio host), and Louis Theroux (1.97M followers, documentary filmmaker, journalist, and broadcaster) got showed unauthorized messages this way.



Not a new issue

Insinia's experiment is not the only one of this kind. Researchers at The AntiSocial Engineer cybersecurity consultancy firm showed the same weakness in Twitter's SMS-based feature on December 22, when they published a tweet on the Twitter account of a publication without needing credentials or authentication.

Richard De Vere says that anyone who has the target's phone number can pull this trick using an SMS spoofing service.

He writes in a blog post on Monday that cybercriminals know about this weakness "and they are not shy of targeting brands." The researcher also points to news articles as old as 2007 and 2009 that brought this problem to public attention, signaling that Twitter is also aware.

Users have no good alternative

Accounts with a massive number of followers have always been a valuable target for a wide variety of attackers. They could use the method demonstrated by Insinia in disinformation campaigns, to distribute malware, or for socially engineering via direct messages.

Avoiding this risk is easy - just remove the phone number from your Twitter account. Unfortunately, doing so also disables the two-factor authentication (2FA) protection, which makes it more difficult for an attacker to get access to your account even if they know the login password.

The mobile carrier also plays a part, since not all of them support the Twitter over SMS feature.

Godfrey, like many other security people, says that 2FA should not rely on a phone in the first place; an authentication app or multi-factor authentication solutions are a much better way to increase security.
