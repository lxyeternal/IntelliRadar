
	   


A hacking group known as CryptoCore has pulled off cryptocurrency heists worth $70 million, but research indicates that it may be an estimated value of over $200 million since 2018.

According to a new report by cybersecurity firm ClearSky, a hacking group called CryptoCore, aka Dangerous Password” or “Leery Turtle”, targets cryptocurrency exchanges by conducting spear-phishing campaigns against employees and executives.

"The key goal of CryptoCore’s heists is to gain access to cryptocurrency exchanges’ wallets, be it general corporate wallets or wallets belonging to the exchange’s employees. For this kind of operation, the group begins with an extensive reconnaissance phase against the company, its executives, officers and IT personnel," the ClearSky report states.

Source: ClearSky

While it is not completely clear where this hacking group is based, ClearSky believes the threat actors have links to the East European region, Ukraine, Russia, or Romania.

Targeted spear-phishing attacks

CryptoCore goes through a reconnaissance phase to identify the personal email accounts for the cryptocurrency exchange's executives when conducting an attack.

"The spear-phishing is typically carried out by impersonating a high-ranking employee either from the target organization or from another organization (e.g. advisory board) with connections to the targeted employee. After gaining initial foothold, the group’s primary objective is obtaining access to the victim’s password manager account. This is where the keys of crypto-wallets and other valuable assets – which will come handy in lateral movement stages – are stored," the report states.


Attack kill chain (Click to see larger image)
Source: ClearSky

These spear-phishing attacks are customized for the target by using domains impersonating affiliated organizations, emails that impersonate executives or affiliated companies,  and malicious .LNK and documents spread via emails.


Example spear-phishing email
Source: ClearSky

The distributed documents pretend to be protected files that need a password to open them. This password is located in a Password.txt.lnk file that, when opened, will execute VBS scripts to download other malicious files.

These VBS scripts are used as a backdoor into the victim's computer, as illustrated by the CryptoCore's TTP diagram from JPCERT's research.


CryptoCore's TTPs
Source: JPCERT

Once CryptoCore has successfully infected a victim, the attackers will use the backdoor to attempt to steal keys to crypto-wallets that are commonly stored in password managers.

ClearSky also suspects the group is using mimikatz on breached computers to collect Windows credentials for the network's domain.

These credentials would then allow the attackers to spread laterally throughout the network as they search for and steal the keys for cryptocurrency wallets.

Once multi-factor authentication is removed from the exchange wallets, the threat actors immediately access them and transfer the cryptocurrency to wallets under their control.

CryptoCore is not alone in its targeting of cryptocurrency exchanges.

In March 2020, the US Dept of Justice sanctioned two Chinese nationals for laundering cryptocurrency stolen during an attack by the North Korean cybercrime group known as the Lazarus Group.
