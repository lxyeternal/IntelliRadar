
	   


Android phones can come with built-in malware and backdoors due to insufficient verification and vetting. Millions of smartphones were infected this way.

Cybercriminals may take advantage of the oversight to place their malicious code straight from the building stage by poisoning the supply chain.

Different approval tiers

Certified Android devices, which come with Google apps pre-installed, use approved build images for the mobile operating system. They are required to go through more rigorous testing before release to consumers, that checks if the Android security and permissions model are respected, and the OS has the latest security updates.

Their products are just Android-compatible, meaning the devices comply with the Android Compatibility Definition Document (CDD) and undergo a security review for harmful behavior in binaries and/or the framework.

Fighting back is typically done with the help of the Google Play Protect (GPP) component that is built into Android specifically to combat malware. When active on a device, GPP can disable malicious applications.

A manufacturer can pre-install any app on their device and this number can get as high as 400, said Maddie Stone, a Google Project Zero security researchers in a presentation at the Black Hat security conference.

Despite the malware verification, malicious apps can still make it onto user devices. It is then up to the manufacturer to release a fix and to Google to warn users about the risk.

Crooks see an opportunity in this model because if they can convince one manufacturer to include malicious code, they can reach thousands of users.

Botnet of 7.4 million devices

Stone presented the case of the Chamois botnet that ran premium SMS fraud, click fraud, app installation fraud; another capability was to load arbitrary modules on the phone, so it could deliver a wider range of threats.



The malware was distributed as an SDK to third-party developers, who unwittingly included it in their app believing it was an advertising library, a mobile payment solution, or some sort of monetization component.

Through this method, the Chamois operators were able to affect over 250 OEMs (original equipment manufacturer) and infect 7.4 million devices in March 2018.



What users saw on their phones was a pre-installed app that could load the Chamois backdoor, the Snowfox trojan and click fraud, among other payloads.

Stone presented the Chamois operation at the Kaspersky Security Analyst Summit in April, detailing the complexity of the malware, the flexibility of the operation, and how Google was able to fight back.

Unintended backdoors

Chamois is just one example of how Android phones can reach consumers in a compromised state straight from the factory. In another case study, Stone talked about two instances where diagnostics software could be leveraged to execute arbitrary code remotely.

In one case it was a complex software had been left in production builds by accident. It had a pre-installed app, a modified SELinux policy, and a custom kernel driver.



The backdoor did not stay open for too long, though. On devices with GPP enabled, it took 72 hours until the app started to be disabled. 

According to Google, new devices with the same issue were rejected from the approval process. In two weeks, the OEM had over-the-air updates ready for 70% of the 6 million users affected; another two weeks later the OEM had covered all users, Stone said.

The other incident involving diagnostics software is another backdoor example. In this case, the software was used for remote management of a large fleet of devices and the problem was a vulnerability (CVE-2018-14825) that allowed remote code execution.

The temporary fix for this one until a patch became available was to protect the Service with a permission, without even touching the code.

"All of the executable code is exactly the same between the backdoor version and the fixed version," Stone wrote in her presentation slides.

Again, Google rejected the approval of new devices that had the issue. The OEM prepared the over-the-air updates in six weeks.

Misconfigured security settings

Oversight seems to be a common problem with Android OEMs. Stone also mentioned misconfigured security settings, which allowed pre-installed apps that used official APIs (signed with the OEM platform key) to disable GPP, stripping the device of its innate protection.

This was possible because detecting malicious behavior on Android used to focus on malicious user-space apps; settings would be changed via the shell and did not have access to the settings APIs.

OEMs used this to include code that disabled GPP temporarily in order to bypass a consent dialog that disrupted automatic fleet provisioning, Stone explained. In most cases, the code attempted to re-enable GPP.

The behavior was fixed by requiring OEMs to no longer use it and through a new AOSP ManagedProvisioning app released in January 2019, 60 days from discovery.

Other vendors took 90 days to implement but Google made a modification that shut this window of opportunity that malicious apps could have used for privilege escalation to disable GPP.

Google Play Protect relies on machine learning to identify harmful behavior and feeds the more complex results to human analysis. It checks over 50 billion apps on a daily basis, according to its website.
