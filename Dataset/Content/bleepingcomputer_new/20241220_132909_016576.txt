
	   


Members of a pornography website targeted dozens of Australian schools so they could view and swap graphic sexual images of female students and other non-consenting women.

News.com reports that members of the site have posted and shared more than 2,000 images since December 2015. Those "wins" (a colloquial term for nude photos) made their way to the forum after users (mainly teenage boys) nominated a specific high school or region along with the full names of girls they were "hunting." Other members then "contributed" by looking up additional personal information about the targeted women, including their physical addresses, phone numbers, and links to their social media profiles.

In some cases, users issued bounties for a win. One member offered to trade 300 images for a single win of a victim he was hunting.

In total, members of the site have targeted at least 70 schools.

News.com does not mention how site members obtained nude photos of their victims. Those with more technical expertise might have obtained the images by infecting their victims with a remote access Trojan like BlackShades and demanding sexual content in exchange for the return of their "slaved" computer.

Victims have responded by asking that their pictures be taken down. Most of them are ignored, some have been laughed at and ridiculed, and others have been "hunted" themselves.

Sharna Bremner from End Rape on Campus says such behavior represents one thing only: an uncaring will to exercise power over another human being.


"They are hunting women and girls who live in their area and sorting them according to geography. It’s the idea of proximity and accessibility that is considered arousing. The thrill is not just that they might see the girl who sits next to them in math class, it’s also that they can put in an order for the girl from math class. What these boys are really getting off on is the sense of power they feel over these girls, and the idea that they can own and obtain them like objects."




If the site members' treatment weren't bad enough, Australian National University Cyber crime expert Professor Roderic Broadhurst notes that these victims' privacy might be forever violated:


"If those images sit on a database in a foreign jurisdiction, where you don’t have mutual legal assistance, how are we to recover them or stop them moving around. These young women possibly have to live with the fact these images are out there forever, there’s nothing they can do."


But that doesn't mean nothing can be done. On 17 August, the Australian Federal Police published a statement in which it explains it is working with domestic and international law enforcement agencies to determine how to proceed against the site. State police forces are also launching their own investigations.

Anyone who thinks someone posted a photo of them to the site should notify the police.
