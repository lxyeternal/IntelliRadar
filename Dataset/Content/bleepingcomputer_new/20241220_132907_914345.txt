They result from more than 100,000 data breaches and over five billion of them are unique.

Most are from consumers but advertisements for corporate accounts that unlock key systems are also a common occurrence on cybercriminal markets, informs a report from data loss detection firm Digital Shadows.

Login pairs from accounts for non-financial services (cable, social media, streaming, VPN services, file sharing, video games, adult) are the cheapest and cybercriminals give away many of them. Those for sale have an average price of $15.43.

From advertisements analyzed by data loss detection firm Digital Shadows, one in four offers accounts related to banking and other financial services. These come at a higher average price, $70.91 each.



However, a confirmed balance for an online banking account, availability of personally identifiable information, and freshness can drive the price up to $500.

In many cases, cybercriminals use them for money laundering to cover their tracks, or for cash-out schemes.


			

			 
			
			
			

			 
			
		As expected, the most expensive accounts are for domain administrator access. These are auctioned to get the best price because they provide the highest levels of trust and control on a network. The average, in this case, is $3,139 but the price can go up to $120,000.

There are dozens of offers and victim company descriptions in these ads include “petrochemical,” “cybersecurity,” “petroleum,” “big university,” as well as various states and governments.

Account takeover tools

Successful account takeover (ATO) attacks have benefits that revolve around identity theft. They often provide access to personal information and monetization can take multiple forms.

From selling the data directly and gaining access to other accounts via credential stuffing, to renting access to accounts, using the data to create synthetic identities, and for defrauding, cybercriminals have a wide array of methods to make money.

The only obstacle in their way is that credentials rarely come in plain text these days and many services check for fingerprint data to identify unauthorized login attempts.

Several markets exist that rent access to an account without triggering an alarm. Some have botnets of info-stealing malware that collect fingerprint data (cookies, IP addresses, time zones) that once injected, make it look like the legitimate owner logged in.



Depending on the type of access they want to rent, cybercriminals can pay less than $10 to log into an account for a limited period using the victim’s fingerprint data.

According to Digital Shadow’s report, Genesis Market is the most popular. It is not without competition, though. UnderWorld Market (formerly RichLog) and Tenebris are in the same business.



Tools and services for cracking passwords and for determining other accounts for which they are valid are also part of successful account compromises.

Digital Shadows calculated that 45.99% of the password hashes they collected used MD5, which makes it trivial to revert them to the original form with today’s processing power.

Brute forcing their way in is a popular method and many of them don’t have a name. Others seem to have gained enough popularity to deserve a label, like Hydra login cracker. Some of these tools are for specific online services:



With lists of credentials from a data breach, threat actors can easily deploy credential stuffing (credential reuse) attacks that provide access to more accounts from the same user and a chance to collect more personal information.

Sentry MBA is a longtime favorite for credential stuffing attacks. It comes with features that bypass some security defenses like IP location or rate-limiting. This allows it to try combinations of millions of usernames and passwords to find valid logins for targeted websites.


Source: Shape Security

Another tool widely discussed on cybercriminal forums is OpenBullet, a website testing suite that can run requests on a target web application. It’s open-source nature, customization options, and low CPU usage, along with included tools for parsing and scraping make it an attractive choice.

Protecting against ATO attacks is an easy task for normal users, who can pick strong, unique passwords and enable two–factor authentication (2FA) on services that support it. This does not eliminate the risk completely but makes it infeasible to attackers as the reward is not worth the resources.

Companies, on the other hand, have a more difficult task as there are multiple aspects to consider. 2FA may not be sufficient when the threat is a motivated actor, so multi-factor authentication (MFA) should be implemented.

This measure alone is far from enough and points of failure may emerge with unpatched systems, repositories with sensitive data, and insufficient employee security awareness.
