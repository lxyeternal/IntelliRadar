
	   


Security researcher Marcus Hutchins pled guilty on Wednesday to writing malware and aiding with its distribution with the help of a partner.

Hutchins is better known for his pivotal role in breaking WannaCry's spread across the world and his online persona MalwareTech where he interacts with the information security community by helping those getting into the field, disseminating information about new threats, and releasing tutorials on how to analyze malware.

Hutchins was arrested at the Las Vegas airport on August 2, 2017, on his way home to the U.K., after attending the Black Hat and DEF CON security conferences as a security researcher.

Filed on Friday, the guilty plea deal is for Count One and Count Two of a total of ten charges provided in a superseding indictment from the U.S. prosecutors.



These refer to developing malware (UPAS-Kit and Kronos banking trojans) and helping with its distribution in partnership with a co-conspirator known "Vinny," "VinnyK," "Aurora 123," "Gone with the Wind," "Cocaine," and "Jack of All Trades." These activities took place between July 2012 and September 2015, according to court documents.

Each of the two charges carries a maximum sentence of five years in prison, up to $250,000 in fines, one year of supervised release, and a $100 special assessment. In total, Hutchins risks 10 years in prison and $500,000 in fines. Following this plea deal, the rest of the counts will be dismissed in court upon sentencing.

It should be noted that regardless of the result of this agreement, Hutchins is not exempt from further civil or administrative actions, from the U.S. or local governments.

In a public statement on his blog, the researcher says that he regrets his actions prior to his career in the cybersecurity field and accepts full responsibility for his mistakes.


"Having grown up, I’ve since been using the same skills that I misused several years ago for constructive purposes. I will continue to devote my time to keeping people safe from malware attacks."


Support is still strong

Although not always admitting it openly, and for good reasons, many security researchers dallied with cybercriminal activity. Oftentimes, there's a drop of blackhat in every professional donning a white hat. This is particularly true with older generations of security experts, who did not have today's information resources; also, they did it at a time when the laws for cyber incidents were too vague or non existent.

Today's internet, however, offers sufficient learning opportunities to reach expert levels without having to break the law and even for free. Hutchins agrees:


There's misconception that to be a security expert you must dabble in the dark side. It's not true. You can learn everything you need to know legally. Stick to the good side.
— MalwareTech (@MalwareTechBlog) April 20, 2019

After his arrest, plenty of researchers rallied to help him out. Even his local Conservative MP, Peter Heaton-Jones, and a dozen more sent letters of support on his behalf. Hutchins has been relying on crowdfunding to cover his legal fees.

Even now, Hutchins has a large crowd in his corner. A cursory look at the replies he got after tweeting his statement about the latest development of the case shows mostly positive comments; you'd have to look harder to find a negative response.






Sucks honestly, you deserve to be back home.
— DEY! (@ronindey) April 19, 2019





At a closer look, it is clear that most of them are in the computer security field: trainers, malware researchers, penetration testers, reverse engineering, security consultants, nerds.

Redeeming himself

After giving up criminal life, Hutchins dedicated his skills to fighting malware threats and applied for a position with UK's intelligence agency, the General Communications Headquarters (GCHQ) but went for a better deal from U.S.- based cybersecurity company Kryptos Logic, who recruited him after seeing his analysis of the Kelihos botnet).

Even before his application, Hutchins published technical articles that showed his reverse-engineering skills, often revealing the tricks used in various malware strains and their components; and offering details on how to fight them.

In a post in 2013 about the imminent leak of the source code for Carberp banking malware, Hutchins wrote this:

"Nothing good comes from leaks like this. AV companies get a massive surge of infected users and spin-off bots are usually created.[...] I guess we can only hope that major antivirus vendors are able to upgrade their software in order to deal with this threat, before more damage is done. Also, the first 5 people to ask me where to get the source will receive a virtual slap in the face (all expenses paid) and my everlasting disapproval."

Even after getting arrested, he continued to add input for fighting cybercrime: identifying and understanding topology of command and control servers (Emotet), tracking botnets (Hide and Seek), reviewing a reverse-engineering tool (NSA's GHIDRA), and analyzing security vulnerabilities.

All this effort translated into a community of supporters that not only offered words of comfort but also pulled together to pay for his legal fees (after his arrest he was denied working for his employer).

This, along with time already served, may also count when the court gives the sentence, for which there is no date set at the moment.
